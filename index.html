Mitsubishi factory procedure for outdoor unit compressor/accumulator replacement. Licensed HVAC professionals only — refer to full manufacturer instructions before attempting.

1. Recover charge
Both high and low side service ports. Current models may lack L-shaped adapters — use valve fittings instead. Heat may be needed at accumulator/compressor to fully recover charge; never use open flame.
2–5. Access
Remove exterior panels, compressor compartment access cover, and top cover (may require removing blanking cover, transformer box, and lowering the electrical panel depending on model)
6–7. De-braze & unmount
De-braze suction and discharge line joints closest to the compressor (or accumulator joints if replacing that). Remove compressor mounting bolt nuts and anti-vibration bracket — one nut is normally missing by design. Remove crankcase heater/thermistor if equipped.
8–10. Oil check
Pour oil from suction pipe into a graduated container. Less than 1 liter (32 oz) or any foreign matter → consult Mitsubishi rep. Acid-test the oil to determine correct filter core if installing a PAC-SPRFCS filter kit.
11–14. Install & braze
Install new compressor/accumulator, filter kit if used, braze all joints while purging with nitrogen at 0.5–2 PSI, reinstall mounting hardware and crankcase heater
15–20. Pressure test & evacuate
Pressurize to 600 PSI with nitrogen and leak check. Evacuate to 4000 microns, break with nitrogen, re-evacuate to 1500 then 500 microns. Hold 500 microns for 30 min pump time, then 1 hour standing.
21. Recharge
Weigh in charge as liquid to the high side only, never the low side. New accumulator requires oil added to the suction line before the accumulator via a two-port oil charge tank.
22–23. Reset compressor hours
Read/reset via dip switches on the control board — switch sequence differs between J-generation and K/L-generation units. Confirm hours read back to zero after reset.
Field tip: Note the missing mounting nut on step 7 is normal, not a prior tech's mistake — don't waste time hunting for a 4th nut that was never there.

EEVs show up in heat pumps, VRF, inverter mini-splits, and chillers — also called LEV (Mitsubishi), EXV (Carel), or EOSM (Sporlan) depending on manufacturer. Covers the common 480-pulse style valve found in most North American equipment.

How it's controlled
Temperature and pressure sensors let the controller calculate superheat and modulate the valve to hold target SH. Certain modes override normal control — Oil Return and Freeze Protection are common examples
Basic parts
Power head (electromagnetic coil + permanent magnet), threaded shaft, needle. The power head has 4 coil windings that sequentially magnetize to rotate the permanent magnet, driving the needle through 48–60 discrete "steps." Typically clockwise = closed, counterclockwise = open, but confirm against the service manual before manually operating a valve
Startup click test
On startup, an audible click is normal as the valve fully cycles closed and open to re-find its position. A valve that doesn't complete this cycle may have a failed coil or stuck pin — but diagnosing purely by sound is subjective, so confirm with electrical/mechanical testing
If a coil fails
The permanent magnet stops rotating entirely — like pulling a domino out of a line, it stops the whole valve's movement. There's no way to know how open or closed the valve was left once a coil fails
Power head continuity test
Power off, unplug the power head from the controller, identify the common wire, then check resistance from each remaining wire to common. Rule of thumb ~40–50Ω ±10% per winding, though the real range can run 26–750Ω depending on the exact EEV — always confirm the manufacturer's spec. Reading low or OL (open) on any winding means replace the EEV
Mechanical (valve body) test
Use a manual (magnet-driven) or powered EEV tool to drive the needle fully open and closed and confirm free range of motion. Position the tool between the middle and top of the valve body so it aligns with the internal magnet — placing it at the bottom misses the magnet entirely. Confirm full travel via temperature drop across the valve, since temp correlates to pressure/position
Powered tool caution
Avoid unplugging or inserting an EEV connector into a control board while it's still powered — risk of board damage
Field tip: Don't try to diagnose an EEV by clicking sound alone — a coil continuity test plus a mechanical range-of-motion test gives a real answer instead of a guess.

Companion to the general economizer troubleshooting card above — this one goes deeper on the sensors and math behind commissioning one properly. ASHRAE 62.1 governs the fresh-air requirement itself; a common rule-of-thumb design assumption is roughly one person per 200 sq ft.

Why economizers matter beyond "free cooling"
They provide two layers of filtration (the outdoor air filter plus the RTU's own filter) and help meet ventilation requirements, not just save energy — "free cooling" is a bit of a misnomer since fan motors and controls still draw power
Temperature vs enthalpy sensors
Temperature (dry-bulb) sensors only account for sensible heat. Enthalpy (wet-bulb) sensors account for total heat, sensible plus latent. An enthalpy sensor may run the economizer even when dry-bulb temp alone looks unfavorable, if humidity conditions are favorable enough
Sensor availability
Many economizers ship with temperature sensors only; enthalpy sensors are sometimes a field-installed upgrade. Some controls (e.g. Honeywell Jade) read both temperature and enthalpy simultaneously
Full sensor set feeding the controller
Outdoor air enthalpy, return air enthalpy, discharge temp, and mixed air temp
Honeywell A/B/C/D settings
Plot on a psychrometric chart — conditions to the left of the selected curve let the economizer handle cooling, conditions to the right hand cooling to the mechanical system. Hot, dry climates commonly use A or B; humid climates commonly use D (when they use economizers at all)
Damper minimum position — purpose
Keeps a constant minimum amount of fresh air flowing without fully closing the damper, even outside of free-cooling mode
Damper minimum position — formula
% outdoor air = (Return air temp − Mixed air temp) ÷ (Return air temp − Outdoor air temp) × 100
Worked example
Outdoor air 40°F, return air 74°F, mixed air 70°F → (74−70) ÷ (74−40) = 4/34 ≈ 12% outdoor air — set the damper minimum position to roughly 12%
Setting it — analog controls
A labeled "Min Pos" potentiometer, adjusted with a screwdriver. Test at fully open (100%) and fully closed (0%) to confirm the damper mechanics work independently of the setpoint itself
Setting it — electronic controls
Set the percentage digitally (e.g. Honeywell Jade), often with a built-in "Damper Open"/"Damper Closed" checkout test in the controller menu. Carrier SystemVu can set minimum position per fan speed
Field tip: Proper commissioning of an economizer is a sensor-and-math exercise as much as a mechanical one — confirm the minimum position calculation against actual sensor readings rather than trusting a factory default.

Systematic fuse-isolation method for a resettable low-voltage fuse that keeps tripping.

1. Verify power source
Check transformer secondary (24–28 VAC). No 24V → check primary, repair/replace transformer.
2. Remove all thermostat wires
Reset fuse, power up. Trips → short is inside the unit (go to step 3). Holds → short is in the stat/cable, reconnect one wire at a time.
3. Isolate components one at a time
Disconnect (in order): gas valve, high-limit/aux safeties, economizer/OA damper harness, blower relay coil, condenser contactor coil. Reset fuse before each test. Fuse holds after disconnecting X → X is shorting.
4. Still trips with everything disconnected
Inspect harness for rubbed wires on blower deck, pinched wires near gas valve bracket, wires touching heat exchanger, or burned transformer secondary touching chassis.
5. Confirm
Reconnect components one at a time until fuse trips again — the last one reconnected is the fault.

TXVs are misdiagnosed constantly, usually because a tech fixates on suction pressure alone. Low suction plus adding refrigerant that doesn't raise it looks like a failed valve — but the valve is often just doing exactly what it's designed to do (close further as more liquid becomes available), while the real problem is airflow or load.

Step 1 — confirm liquid supply
Check subcooling first. In theory 1°F is the minimum for a full line of liquid, but in reality confirm the factory-specified subcool, usually around 10°F
Step 2 — confirm pressure differential
Want at least 100 PSI minimum difference between liquid line pressure and desired evaporator pressure. Low ambient conditions dropping head pressure too far can starve the valve of the differential it needs to function
Step 3 — check evaporator outlet superheat
Most A/C systems maintain 6–14°F superheat at the evap outlet once subcool and pressure differential are confirmed adequate. In range = the valve is doing its job, not bad
Below 6°F superheat
Possible overfeeding (failed open) — double check thermometer/gauge calibration first before condemning the valve
Above 14°F superheat with proper subcool/differential confirmed
Failed closed (underfeeding). Check for a blocked inlet screen before assuming the valve itself is bad — freeze the coil intentionally or use thermal imaging to find the actual point of temperature/pressure drop
Overfeeding first check
Bulb insulation, placement, and strapping — if the bulb isn't properly sensing the suction line, the valve stays too far open regardless of anything else
Most common real causes of failure
Contaminants or moisture reaching the valve and sticking/restricting it, bulb tube rub-outs, or an external equalizer without a core depressor installed on a Schrader port
Refrigeration cross-check
Same principle as a sight glass check on a split system — confirm no kinked lines or plugged driers before condemning the valve
Field tip: If you're chasing low suction and adding refrigerant makes the TXV close further without suction climbing, stop — that's the valve doing its job correctly. Go check airflow and load before touching the valve.

Compressor valve wear
Amps below RLA despite otherwise normal charge signs. Gauges will confirm: suction high for the load, head low for ambient, SH high/SC low despite adequate charge. Low compression ratio is the tell — see Compression Ratio under the Reference tab.
Non-condensables
Amps at/above RLA, all lines proportionally hot to the touch. Gauges will confirm: head high for ambient, and condenser sat temp reads higher than SC/ambient can explain.
Dirty/restricted condenser airflow
Fan amps/RPM confirmed correct but the condenser coil is visibly dirty or blocked → points to coil face restriction, not the fan. Gauges will confirm: head high for ambient.
Field tip: If condenser airflow independently confirms good and the ambient-to-sat-temp delta is still abnormal, suspect non-condensables over a dirty coil.

CLO stands for Current-Sensing Lock Out (also called Compressor Lock Out). It prevents restart of the compressor/unit after a safety trip until power is momentarily interrupted to the CLO. Applies to Carrier 38/48/50 series units.

Normal operating condition
18–30 VAC at Terminals 1 & 2, with 2.5A through the current sensor loop — relay stays de-energized
Trip condition
Current drops to 0.5A → relay energizes within 1 second, opens N.C. contact between 2 & 3, closes N.C. contact between 2 & X (alarm signal)
What trips it
HPS (high pressure switch), LPS (low pressure switch), or FPT (freeze protection thermostat) opening in the C1 compressor contactor circuit
Field tip: A compressor that won't restart after a nuisance trip, with no obvious fault present, is often just waiting on the CLO to be reset by cycling power — check the CLO before condemning the compressor or contactor.

Unloaders reduce compressor capacity by isolating a cylinder bank from the suction manifold, letting the unloaded cylinders spin without pumping refrigerant. Two control types on Carrier reciprocating compressors: electric (remote signal to a solenoid) and pressure-operated (driven by suction/discharge differential).

Electric — loaded (de-energized)
Valve passageways aligned for loaded conditions. No manual adjustment required.
Electric — unloaded (energized)
Solenoid opens the capacity control valve port, venting discharge gas from behind the unloader piston back to suction — isolates that cylinder bank, no compression
Pressure-operated — loaded
Suction pressure overcomes control set point spring → poppet valve closes → discharge gas builds against unloader piston → bank runs fully loaded
Pressure-operated — unloaded
Suction pressure drops below set point → diaphragm snaps, opens poppet → discharge gas vents to suction side → cylinder bank isolated
Control set point range
0–86 psig, adjustable. Each full turn CCW on the adjustment nut lowers load-up point ~7.2 psi
Pressure differential range
6–16 psi between load-up and unload points. Each full turn CW on the adjustment screw increases differential ~0.8 psi
Startup behavior
On pressure-operated units, controlled cylinders don't load until suction/discharge differential reaches approx 25 psig
Field check
Solenoid should give an audible click when actuated (electric type). No click with 24V present — suspect solenoid coil.
Field tip: Cylinder head assemblies include the capacity control valve — for electrically actuated heads, the solenoid coil is purchased separately. Confirm valve plate gasket thickness (0.32" for 06ER/EY refrigeration models, 0.07" for all others) before reassembly.

Residential techs moving to 5-20 ton light commercial rooftops share a lot with residential equipment, but there's more to check on the bigger stuff.

Fresh air filters
Often metal mesh — wash regularly with a hose and degreaser, especially near exhaust hoods
Belt tension
Tight enough not to slip or vibrate excessively, no tighter — over-tight belts cause high blower amps, breakage, stretching, and excessive bearing wear. The 1/2" deflection rule is a rough guide at best since it ignores belt length and deflection force; a proper tension tool gives a real number
Pulley alignment
Align both pulley centers (left-right) and camber (angle). Misalignment usually traces back to a slipped motor base bolt or a recently adjusted/replaced pulley. Causes thrown/damaged belts, damaged bearings, noise. Worn pulleys (lost shape, grooved sides) cause improper airflow and shorter belt life even when alignment is correct
Condenser splitting
Multi-row condensers often need to be split apart to clean completely — frequency depends on location and airborne debris
Phase balance (3-phase)
Check voltage leg to leg on all three legs. Imbalance over 2% is detrimental, over 4% is unacceptable — even small imbalances cause motor heat and failure. Many 3-phase systems already have phase monitoring controls installed to catch this automatically
Field tip: New commercial techs tend to over-tighten belts out of habit from residential work — if you're chasing high blower amps or premature bearing wear on a rooftop, check belt tension before anything else.

Two compressors sharing one circuit (three = a trio) — common in RTUs, chillers, DOAS, and data center cooling to hit minimum modulation requirements without the expense of a single variable-speed compressor.

Why use them
Splits load across two compressors so the circuit can turn down further (e.g. to 25% of full capacity) than one single-speed compressor could alone — helps meet efficiency standards like IEER/IPLV
OEL (oil equalization line)
Connects the two compressor sumps below the compressors so oil balances between them. Must be level — sloped OEL piping starves one compressor of oil and floods the other, causing premature failure. Copeland tandem-ready compressors ship with spacers to keep it level
TPTL (two-phase transfer line)
Larger line that equalizes both oil and refrigerant, usually with its own sight glass for checking oil level. Most Copeland trios use this. Combined with the OEL, this is a "three-pipe" design
GEL (gas equalization line)
Located near the top of the compressor shell, equalizes pressure between compressors. If present, it's a "four-pipe" design — common on the smallest tandem pairings where a sight glass won't fit
Replacing just one failed compressor
Compressors under 7" diameter or under 10 HP generally require full tandem replacement — most distributors don't stock tandem-ready compressors that small. Look for an oil equalization port and sight glass to confirm a compressor is tandem-ready before ordering a single replacement
When to replace the whole tandem anyway
Contamination from a catastrophic failure of one compressor can compromise the other, since they share oil and refrigerant — even a 9"+ compressor might warrant full tandem replacement in that case
Field tip: Never replace a tandem-ready compressor with a standard (non-tandem) compressor even if the ports look close — a non-tandem compressor has no oil equalization port and cannot be piped into an existing OEL/TPTL/GEL setup.

Prevents liquid refrigerant from migrating into the compressor crankcase during the off-cycle and diluting the oil. A missing or failed crankcase heater is a leading cause of premature compressor failure — and it's not just a cold-climate problem (a hot attic keeping the indoor coil warm while the outdoor unit cools rapidly at sunset causes the same migration in Florida).

What happens without one
Liquid refrigerant floods the crankcase and dilutes the oil. At next startup, the sudden pressure drop makes it boil violently into foam — this causes bearing washout (foam forced out, starving bearings of lubrication) and slugging (incompressible liquid drawn into cylinders/scrolls, breaking valves or shattering scroll sets)
Scroll compressors
Have compliance mechanisms that let scrolls separate slightly to handle small amounts of liquid, but they're not immune — a crankcase heater may be skippable only below a specific charge limit per the manufacturer's data. Never add a hard start kit to a scroll that's stalling on liquid migration; the extra torque can force it to start against a slug and break the scroll set
Variable-speed compressors
Often don't need an external heater at all — the VFD sends small DC current through the motor windings, generating up to 50W of heat directly
Wrap-around (belly-band)
Most common type, straps around the compressor housing. Easy field install/replacement, even heating, but less efficient than internal heaters
Immersion (well) style
Inserts into a dedicated port, heats the oil directly. More efficient and faster, but only works on compressors designed with a well
Installation
Install as low on the shell as possible without covering the bottom weld, perfectly horizontal, snug contact along the full length, avoid other weld seams (creates hot spots). Torque the clamp to 20–25 in-lb. Never power a heater in free air before it's installed — it will burn out
Pre-startup requirement
On a new install or after extended shutdown, energize the heater a minimum of 24 hours before first start — this drives off migrated liquid from the oil, not just warms it. The first start is the most critical one a compressor gets; don't skip this
Diagnosing a failed heater
Visual: cracks, burned spots, frayed leads. Temperature: shell should feel slightly warm when energized, not hot. Resistance: OL means open/failed element (target Ω = voltage² ÷ wattage). Ground: any reading other than OL indicates a short to ground
Wiring method to watch for
When replacing a single-pole contactor with a double-pole, the crankcase heater can lose its power source since a double-pole interrupts both legs. Fix: jumper L2 to T2 to mimic the continuous-voltage behavior the heater was relying on
Field tip: A crankcase heater is one of the most legitimate low-cost upsells in the trade — cheap insurance against a multi-thousand-dollar compressor replacement, and a mark of a thorough tech.

R-410A equipment with a GWP over 700 stopped being manufactured January 1, 2025, and installation of new R-410A split systems becomes illegal January 1, 2026 (chillers and other specialized equipment have later deadlines). Scroll compressor design had to change to handle the new refrigerants.

Common misconception
A2Ls do NOT contain R-290 (propane, an A3 refrigerant). R-32 is a pure refrigerant on its own; R-454B is a blend of 68.9% R-32 and 31.1% R-1234yf — no propane in either
Why compressor design changed
R-32 runs higher discharge temperatures than R-410A, requiring a more viscous oil (POE-46 instead of POE-36) to keep from breaking down under the extra heat. R-454B has minor glide (1–2.5°F) requiring some design accommodation, though nowhere near as much as older R-407C blends (up to 11.5°F)
Copeland model number nomenclature
Traditional A1 scroll models start with Z. A2L/flammable-refrigerant models start with Y — specifically YA for R-454B, YP for R-32
R-410A replacement compressors still available
The manufacturing ban applies to complete systems, not individual parts — Copeland's new LXE line (ZPLXE single-stage, ZPSLXE two-stage) replaces legacy ZP-prefix models for service work, with 80 LXE models covering 277 legacy models
Service practice changes
Minimal if you were already doing things right — nitrogen pressure testing and deep vacuum go from best practice to required, but R-32/R-454B operating pressures are close enough to R-410A that gauge readings and targets don't shift dramatically
Field tip: Don't assume "A2L" means propane is involved — check the actual refrigerant name (R-32, R-454B, etc.) before making assumptions about flammability handling or charge limits.

Discharge line temp is easy to check (no need to break into the sealed system) and gives useful insight into compression ratio and compressor health, especially on heat pumps in winter when suction SH/SC readings are harder to access.

225°F ceiling
If discharge line temp at the compressor exceeds 225°F, that's a problem — it corresponds to an internal compressor temp over 300°F, where oil starts breaking down ("225 stay alive" rule of thumb)
PVE oil note
PVE oil, common in VRF/VRV and ductless equipment, starts breaking down at 247°F — well below the 300°F internal-temp threshold, so VRF/ductless systems have less margin
Typical normal range
Roughly 160–180°F is a commonly seen discharge line temp on properly functioning A/C systems in warm-climate summer conditions — useful as a rough sanity check, not a hard target
Compression ratio drives discharge temp
High head or low suction pressure raises compression ratio, which raises discharge temp. Low suction pressure also means lower-density refrigerant, which cools the compressor less effectively (less mass flow through the crankcase) — both push discharge temp up
Suction line insulation
An uninsulated or poorly insulated suction line raises suction superheat arriving at the compressor, which can also increase discharge line temp independent of charge or metering issues
Discharge superheat
Discharge line temp minus discharge saturation temp at that pressure. Runs much higher than suction SH — a variable-speed VRV compressor may normally range 32–72°F depending on speed
High discharge superheat (up to 100+°F)
Check suction superheat first to confirm adequate vapor supply/charge. If suction SH is fine, the extra heat is coming from friction inside the compressor itself — often low oil level or mechanical wear, not a charge problem
Low discharge superheat
Indicates liquid compression — liquid or a liquid/vapor mix reaching the compressor washes out oil and can gall scroll surfaces or damage valves. Low suction AND discharge superheat together points to liquid getting back to the compressor
High discharge temp — common causes
Low charge, severe overcharge, low condenser airflow, restricted metering device, or other restrictions (drier, kinked line, clogged screen)
Low discharge temp — common causes
Slight-to-moderate overcharge, low load/airflow, or a compressor not pumping (low compression)
Field tip: High discharge temp with normal suction superheat points at the compressor itself (oil, wear) rather than charge — don't add refrigerant chasing a discharge temp reading without confirming suction SH first.

A heat pump uses the same compression cycle as straight A/C, just run in reverse via a reversing/four-way valve — the coil that's normally the evaporator becomes the condenser, and vice versa.

Y terminal
On a heat pump, Y isn't a "cooling" signal — it energizes the compressor contactor in both heating and cooling. The mode shift is handled separately by the reversing valve solenoid, most commonly a 24V call on O for cooling (less commonly B for heat)
Reversing valve solenoid
A 24V electromagnetic coil on the valve that shifts a small pilot valve, not the main slide directly — system pressure does the actual shifting. Never energize it unless it's properly mounted on the valve, or it can overheat and fail
Pressure needed to shift
Takes roughly 40 PSIG of differential pressure to shift a reversing valve. Below that, it won't shift internally — a compressor that isn't running or has poor compression can cause a valve misdiagnosis when the valve itself is fine
Two metering devices
Most heat pumps have one metering device outdoors (for heat mode) and one indoors (for cool mode), each needing a bypass path (internal or external check valve) for the opposite mode. Some systems pair a TXV indoors with a piston outdoors — don't assume both are the same type
Bi-flow filter drier
Because the liquid line's flow direction reverses between heat and cool mode, a heat pump liquid line needs a bi-flow drier that filters in both directions
High head in heat mode
The indoor coil becomes the condenser in heat mode, so low indoor airflow (dirty filter/coil/blower/ducts) is the first thing to check on abnormally high head pressure
Defrost
When the outdoor coil ices up, the system briefly switches to cooling mode, shuts off the condenser fan, and runs aux heat (where equipped) until defrost completes. The valve-shift noise and visible steam are normal and can trigger nuisance customer calls
Suction pressure in heat mode
Since the evaporator is outside in heat mode, suction pressure/saturation drops as outdoor temp drops — this raises compression ratio and reduces capacity. Don't overcharge a heat pump on a cold day trying to force suction pressure back up
Reading suction pressure in heat mode
Connect the low-side gauge to the dedicated "common suction" port between compressor and reversing valve — the line normally called "suction" is a high-pressure vapor line in heat mode
Field tip: High head pressure on a heat pump running in heat mode almost always points to indoor airflow first, since the indoor coil is acting as the condenser — check filters and blower before touching charge.

A common nuisance callback: water backing up into the secondary drain pan on straight-cool A/C units and heat pumps, but not on furnaces. The difference comes down to coil/blower arrangement and drain-side pressure.

Why furnaces don't have this problem
Coil-over-blower arrangement (typical furnace) puts the drain under positive pressure, which pushes air out of the drain
Why heat pumps/retrofit air handlers do
Coil-under-blower arrangement (most heat pumps and many retrofit straight-cool air handlers) puts the drain under negative pressure, which sucks air back into the drain. A trap that's too shallow lets that air back in and the condensate backs up
Trap sizing rule of thumb
Trap depth (inches) ≈ 2 × return-side static pressure (inches w.c.). Example: 0.5" w.c. static → 1" deep trap. 0.7" w.c. static → 1.4" deep trap. Always follow the manufacturer's spec over this rule of thumb where one is given
Static pressure isn't fixed
Filter restriction (especially on ECM-blower systems) can raise static pressure over time — don't undersize a trap based on a low return static reading on day one
Bigger is generally fine
Main requirement is the trap outlet sitting at least one pipe dimension lower than the inlet. Residential HVAC rarely needs a trap deeper than 2". If return static ever reaches 1" w.c., there are bigger problems to address than trap depth
Field tip: This is a negative-pressure problem specifically — it won't show up on positively pressurized furnace drains. Don't forget to cap the cleanouts once the trap is sized correctly.

Same basic refrigeration cycle as any DX system — the difference is the evaporator chills water (or glycol) instead of cooling air directly, and that water is then piped out to air handlers/fan coils elsewhere in the building.

Reciprocating
Older/smaller chillers, largely phased out on new equipment but still common in the field. Piston-driven, multiple cylinders, capacity staged by unloading cylinder banks (see Compressor unloaders card under RTU/Package Units)
Scroll
Small-to-mid tonnage chillers, often multiple scrolls manifolded together for capacity steps. Quiet, few moving parts, common on air-cooled packaged chillers

Mid-to-large tonnage. Twin rotating screws compress refrigerant continuously — smooth capacity modulation via a sliding slide valve, no on/off cylinder banks. Oil management is critical (oil separator, oil cooler)

Large tonnage (100+ tons), common in big commercial/campus plants. Impeller-driven, most efficient per ton at full load. Often runs refrigerants below atmospheric pressure at the evaporator (R-123, R-1233zd) — requires a purge unit to remove air/non-condensables that leak in

Prevents the compressor from running with no/low water flow through the evaporator — a flowing evaporator is what keeps the refrigerant from freezing solid. Nuisance trips are usually a dirty strainer, air in the system, or a failed pump, not the switch itself
Low temp / freeze protection cutout
Second layer of protection behind the flow switch — trips on low leaving water temp or low refrigerant temp/pressure before the evaporator can freeze and split. Never bypass this to keep a unit running

Low water flow, low charge, or a failing TXV/EEV all drop evaporator temp below 32°F at the tube wall — ice expands and can crack copper tubes, turning a service call into a full evaporator replacement
High/low pressure safeties
Same purpose as any DX system — protect the compressor from operating outside its envelope. On larger machines these are often electronic (transducer + control board) rather than mechanical switches

Since the evaporator runs below atmospheric pressure, air and moisture leak in over time rather than refrigerant leaking out. The purge unit periodically pulls this air off and vents it, keeping non-condensables from building up and killing capacity/efficiency

This is the concept that makes the approach/wet-bulb numbers elsewhere in this section make sense. A cooling tower doesn't cool water by blowing air past it like a condenser coil — it cools water by evaporating a small portion of it.

Why evaporation cools
Turning liquid water into vapor takes a large amount of heat (latent heat of vaporization) — that heat gets pulled from the remaining water, cooling it. This is the same principle as sweat cooling skin
Why wet bulb, not dry bulb, is the limit
Because the process depends on evaporation, a tower's performance is limited by how much moisture the air can still absorb — that's what wet-bulb temperature measures. A tower can cool water close to (but never below) the entering air's wet-bulb temp, regardless of the dry-bulb (regular thermometer) temp outside
Why this matters in humid weather
High humidity means wet-bulb temp is close to dry-bulb temp, so the air can't absorb much more moisture — tower performance drops even if it doesn't feel especially hot outside. This is normal, not a fault
Water loss
Evaporation constantly loses water to the atmosphere — this is why towers need continuous makeup water and a float valve, unlike a sealed closed-loop system
Field tip: If a customer asks why the tower "isn't cooling as well" on a humid day, this is usually the answer — check approach temp against wet bulb, not against the air temp on your phone's weather app.
Splash fill
Water splashes over stacked bars/grids, breaking into droplets to increase surface area for evaporation. More open, less prone to clogging, but lower efficiency than film fill
Film fill
Water flows as a thin film over closely spaced PVC sheets. More surface area and more efficient than splash fill, but fouls more easily — needs cleaner water and is more sensitive to scaling/algae
Drift eliminators
Baffles at the air discharge that catch water droplets carried by the airflow, so the tower loses water only to evaporation, not to droplets blowing out with the exhaust air. Damaged/missing eliminators show up as excessive water loss and wet surfaces/staining near the tower discharge
Fan drive types
Belt-driven (adjustable via sheave, most common on smaller towers), gear-driven (right-angle gearbox, common on larger induced-draft towers, needs its own oil service), and direct-drive (motor shaft-mounted straight to the fan, fewer wear points)
Induced vs forced draft
Induced draft: fan at the top pulls air up through the fill (most common). Forced draft: fan at the bottom pushes air in — more prone to recirculating warm discharge air back into the intake
VFD fan control
Modulates fan speed to hold a target leaving water temp instead of just cycling on/off — saves energy and reduces belt/bearing wear from constant starts. See Mounted bearing lubrication under Motor & Drive Reference for gearbox/fan shaft bearing service
Field tip: Excessive water loss with no obvious leak is almost always drift eliminators — check them before assuming a piping or basin leak.

Open cooling towers create warm water aerosolized into the air — exactly the conditions Legionella bacteria need to grow and spread. Water treatment on a tower isn't just about protecting equipment, it's a health and safety issue.

Why it matters
Legionnaires' disease spreads through inhaled mist from contaminated water sources — poorly maintained cooling towers are a documented source of outbreaks. Many states/municipalities require registered testing and maintenance plans for open towers
Biocide
Kills bacteria/algae in the water — needs to be maintained at proper levels continuously, not just shocked occasionally
Cycles of concentration / blowdown
As water evaporates, dissolved minerals concentrate in the remaining water. Blowdown (bleeding off some tower water and replacing with fresh makeup) keeps mineral concentration and conductivity from climbing too high and scaling the fill/coils
pH
Typically maintained 7.0–9.0. Out-of-range pH accelerates scaling (high) or corrosion (low)
If a tower has sat idle
Towers that have been offline (seasonal shutdown, unoccupied building) need to be disinfected before restart per manufacturer/local requirements — stagnant warm water is a higher Legionella risk than water in continuous circulation
Field tip: If a building's water treatment program looks neglected (no test records, cloudy/slimy basin water), flag it — this is a liability issue for the customer, not just a maintenance one.
Control method
CV: cycling compressors or modulating coil valves. VAV: adjusting zone damper position to vary airflow
Airflow
CV: constant at all times. VAV: modulated continuously by VAV boxes while supply temp stays relatively constant
Energy efficiency
CV: less efficient, fans run full speed at partial load. VAV: highly efficient, fan speed reduces via VFD
Typical equipment
CV: RTUs, small AHUs, simple systems. VAV: large AHUs, multi-zone buildings
Controls/BAS
CV: usually thermostat-based, minimal BAS. VAV: fully BAS-integrated with static pressure reset, scheduling, trending

A second controlled pathway for fresh air besides economizers — essentially larger versions of the residential HRV/ERV, common where a building needs to meet ASHRAE 62.1 ventilation rates without relying on uncontrolled envelope leakage.

ASHRAE 62.1 outdoor-air fraction (OAF)
Minimum ratio of outdoor air intake to total supply air, varies by building type — e.g. 15% for retail/office, 20–40% for auditoriums/gymnasiums
How they work
A blower assembly pulls in outdoor air and exhausts stale return air, with filtration on the incoming airstream. The outgoing and incoming air cross within a heat exchanger without directly mixing, recovering energy that would otherwise be lost to the exhaust
HRV (Heat Recovery Ventilator)
Sensible heat exchange only, typically via a fixed-plate crossflow surface. Best suited to cold, dry climates — recovers heat from warmer indoor exhaust air to precondition cold incoming outdoor air
ERV (Energy Recovery Ventilator)
Sensible AND latent exchange via a desiccant-coated medium, either fixed-plate or a rotary "enthalpy wheel." Suits milder, moderately humid ("green grass") climates. Not as effective as active dehumidification in very humid climates — some ERVs offer an optional sensible-only mode
Typical ERV parts
Blower, exchange medium (often an enthalpy wheel), filters, digital control box, access panel
Demand-control IAQ integration
Some ERV controls tie into CO2/VOC sensors with an alarm setpoint (e.g. 1200 PPM) and a time delay, letting the BAS bring in fresh air on demand rather than continuously
Climate matching matters
An HRV in a humid climate or an ERV in a very cold, dry climate is the wrong tool — match the unit type to what the building actually needs to manage (sensible only vs sensible + latent)
Field tip: If a commercial client's main concern is contaminant/CO2 control rather than temperature or humidity, an ERV with IAQ sensor integration can bring in fresh air on demand instead of running continuously — churches are a common example of this use case.

Communicating "signal" controls run at low voltage but are far more susceptible to induced electrical noise than a simple on/off control circuit, since they're picking up charges from nearby conductors, electronics, and even lightning transients rather than just switching a load.

What shielded cable does
A metallic jacket surrounds the individual conductors and routes induced charges to ground instead of letting them corrupt the signal
Grounding rule
Bond the shield to a good equipment ground on ONE end only. Grounding both ends risks turning the shield into a ground-fault path and can create a ground loop that generates the very noise you're trying to eliminate
If shielded cable wasn't run
Grounding leftover/unused conductors on one end can sometimes help, but isn't guaranteed — unlike a true shield, spare conductors don't fully surround the ones actually carrying the signal
Manufacturer requirements vary
Some communicating systems require shielded cable, some merely recommend it, others don't address it at all — check the specific system's install documentation rather than assuming
Field tip: A system that throws intermittent communication faults, especially correlating with nearby electrical storms or equipment, is a classic sign of unshielded (or improperly grounded) low-voltage signal wiring picking up induced noise.

Low voltage wiring
Typically ≤50V, most common is 24VAC in HVAC (thermostats, controls, safeties). Carries control/signal current, not load current. "Safer" than line voltage but still causes damage or comm failures if poorly wired. Sensitive to EMI near power lines/motors — run separate from high voltage, shield if needed. Match voltage type (24VAC vs 12/24VDC) — mismatch damages components
Relay basics
Coil (receives the low voltage signal) → armature → contacts (open/close) → spring (returns to rest state). A relay bridges low voltage control to a high voltage load — e.g. a 24V thermostat signal energizes a relay coil, which switches 240V to the compressor
EMR (electromechanical)
Physical contacts — the traditional relay type, visible/audible click on operation
SSR (solid state)
No moving contacts — faster switching, silent, more reliable long-term, higher cost
Latching relay
Holds its position with power removed — power-saving design, common where the load needs to stay set without continuous coil power
Time delay relay
Controlled on/off delay, used in sequencing (e.g. staggering compressor starts, blower-off delay)
Contact rating
Must match the load — underrated contacts arc, weld shut, and fail early
Coil voltage
Must match the control signal exactly, including AC vs DC — a DC coil on an AC signal (or vice versa) won't operate correctly even if the voltage number looks right
Inductive loads
Motors and solenoids need spike protection (snubber circuits) — the collapsing magnetic field on de-energization generates a voltage spike that can damage contacts/electronics without one
Isolation
Relays provide electrical isolation between the control circuit and the load circuit — this is a safety function, not just a switching convenience
Field tip: A relay that hums or chatters instead of pulling in cleanly is usually low coil voltage or a worn/misaligned armature — check coil voltage first before condemning the relay.

Short cycling is equipment turning on and off more than optimal. Never intentionally rapid-cycle equipment to try to "spread out" runtime (some utilities have proposed this for load-shedding) — it's a hard no. Long, steady run times beat frequent short cycles every time.

Safeties & limits
Furnace limit switches, motor overloads, and refrigerant high/low/loss-of-charge pressure switches shut equipment off before a failure. Most have a natural gap between make and break points plus a time delay, which helps prevent rapid re-cycling
Power interruptions
Rapid power cycling (storms, a breaker flipped on/off, a bumped condensate switch) can restart equipment with no time delay — hard on motors and controls, and can occasionally even make a scroll compressor run backward
Oversizing / low load
Equipment sized above the load hits setpoint quickly and short-cycles. Well-designed systems run nearly continuously at peak load — reassure customers that a system that "never shuts off" in extreme weather is a good sign for longevity and efficiency, not a fault, as long as comfort is maintained
Controls design
An on/off dead-band plus a max CPH (cycles per hour) setting balances comfort against short-cycling. Example: 3°F dead-band, cooling CPH of 3, 75°F setpoint → comes on at 76°F, shuts off at 74°F, capped at 3 cycles per hour
Electrical consequences
Voltage sag/dimming lights during motor or compressor start-up, contactor and relay contact wear from repeated arcing, premature hot surface ignitor failure
Mechanical/performance consequences
Compressor oil loss and lubrication issues, poor dehumidification (the coil needs run time to cool down and start condensing moisture), poor efficiency (steady-state performance takes time to reach), radiant discomfort (air temp changes fast but furniture/walls/floors don't), poor ventilation control
Field tip: If a customer complains a system "never shuts off," check comfort and efficiency before assuming a problem — continuous run time at design conditions on a properly sized system is expected, not a fault.

A TXV bulb has no logic of its own — it's charged with refrigerant that follows the same pressure-temperature rules as the rest of the system, and it's this bulb pressure that provides the valve's opening force. Everything about mounting it comes down to one goal: read suction line temperature as accurately as possible.

The 3-way balance
Bulb pressure provides the opening force. Spring pressure and equalizing pressure (accounting for the evaporator's pressure drop) both provide closing force. The valve modulates based on this balance, so an inaccurate bulb reading throws off the whole valve
Clock position rules of thumb
Roughly 1 o'clock for ⅜"–⅝" OD tubing, 3 o'clock for ¾"–1¼" OD — but good contact with the suction line matters more than hitting the exact clock position
Avoid 6 o'clock
Mounting at 6 o'clock risks the bulb picking up oil temperature pooled at the bottom of the line instead of refrigerant temperature, which throws off the superheat reading
Strapping
Use the manufacturer-provided copper strap when possible; any material that conducts heat similarly works if you can't. Never use zip ties or tape — zip ties also wear out over time and lose contact. Mount to a straight section of tubing, not a joint
Vertical tubing
The capillary tube from bulb to powerhead should point UP. Pointed down, most of the liquid refrigerant in the powerhead won't be in the bulb, so it won't respond well to temperature changes
Insulating the bulb
Critical in HVAC applications, where ambient temperature is almost always well above suction line temperature. Less critical in refrigeration, where suction line temp is often closer to ambient already
Protecting from brazing heat
The bulb is heat-sensitive — on R-410A systems it tops out around 167°F. Wrap it in a wet towel or heat-blocking putty (e.g. Refrigeration Technologies WetRag), or just remove it, especially when using a rosebud tip or air-acetylene torch that spreads heat over a wider area
Field tip: Good contact beats precise clock position — if you can't hit the textbook clock position but can get solid contact on a straight section of pipe, take the contact every time.

Adjusting a TXV is a last resort, not a first move — and not every valve can even be adjusted.

Is it even adjustable?
Non-adjustable valves are flat on the bottom. Adjustable ones have a nut/extension covering an adjustable stem. Attempting to adjust a non-adjustable valve can release the entire system charge — confirm before reaching for a wrench
Rule out everything else first
Bulb contact and position (not 6 o'clock), mechanical subcooler operation if present, adequate airflow/load match, and restrictions (dirty filter driers, TXV screens) should all be ruled out before adjusting the stem
Why not just adjust it
Opening the valve to compensate for an unrelated operational problem makes floodback more likely — once someone fixes the real issue later, the valve can slam open and dump liquid into the compressor
Which way to turn
Clockwise increases superheat (tighter spring, less liquid through). Counterclockwise decreases superheat (looser spring, more liquid through). Turn only ½ turn at a time
Testing after each adjustment
Run the system fully buttoned up under typical operating conditions (expected airflow, panels on) before re-measuring and making further adjustments
Don't force it
The adjustment screw should turn with minimal resistance past the initial "unstick." Forcing it, or adjusting too much/too roughly over time, can strip the stem and permanently lock the valve in position — at that point it needs replacement
Where this actually comes up
Adjustable TXVs are more common in mission-critical commercial refrigeration than residential/light commercial HVAC
Field tip: An out-of-range superheat reading at the evaporator outlet is a symptom to investigate, not an invitation to grab a wrench — work the checklist of other causes first.

Equalizers and distributors usually show up together on larger coils, and understanding one helps explain why the other is needed.

What the equalizer actually does
Provides a closing force that references the pressure drop across the evaporator, counteracting the bulb's opening force — NOT there to even out pressure during the off cycle
Internal equalization
Works fine for small coils with a small pressure drop — the reference is simply taken at the TXV's own outlet
Distributors
Small tubes feeding refrigerant into separate rows of a larger evaporator coil. Each tube effectively acts like its own small evaporator with its own pressure drop
Why distributors need an external equalizer
An internally-equalized valve doesn't account for the extra pressure drop the distributor tubes add, so the bulb's superheat reference becomes inaccurate on coils that use them
Bulb-to-equalizer placement
Textbook answer: equalizer connection downstream of the bulb on the suction line. Downstream placement optimizes superheat/load response. Upstream placement instead protects the compressor — if the valve leaks liquid through the equalizer line, the bulb senses the flooding and closes the valve. It's a tradeoff of priorities more than a strict right/wrong
Blocked external equalizer — progression
Initially the valve may read zero evaporator pressure and open too wide (flooding risk). Pressure then gradually climbs and fills the equalizer line (often via off-cycle bleed or nitrogen pressurization), which slams the valve permanently shut with no way to bleed back out
Common causes of a blockage
Solder blockage, or an undepressed Schrader core where the equalizer connects via a Schrader port
Field tip: A TXV that's permanently slammed shut with no charge movement at all is worth checking the external equalizer line for a blockage before condemning the valve itself.

Delta T is one piece of the diagnostic puzzle — not a standalone verdict. The 20°F rule of thumb is a starting point, not a target to force a system into.

Formula
ΔT = T2 − T1, measured across the evaporator (return air temp minus supply air temp)
Probe placement is critical
Take the supply reading just above the air handler, not at a register — leaky, torn, or poorly insulated ducts add unwanted gains between the coil and the register that corrupt the reading. Return reading should be equally representative, not right next to a leak
20°F is a rule of thumb, not universal
Most equipment falls in the 16–20°F range at 400 CFM/ton, but manufacturers vary target delta T by equipment design. Chasing 20°F on a system designed for 15°F just makes things worse
Airflow effect
System capacity (BTU absorption) doesn't change with fan speed — only how much air passes through the coil does. Decreased airflow = high split. Increased airflow = low split. This is predictable and gives you a logical starting point when delta T is off
Humidity effect
High humidity air is less dense (water vapor molecules are lighter than air), meaning less sensible heat capacity per cubic foot, plus latent heat removal (condensing moisture) draws energy away from the sensible heat exchange — both push delta T lower on humid days, even on a correctly operating system
Refrigerant charge
Delta T is NOT a reliable charge-verification test on its own — you can read 20°F on a badly charged system and 12°F on a properly charged one just as easily. Use it alongside suction/head pressure, superheat, and subcool, not instead of them
350 vs 400 CFM/ton systems
In humid climates, some systems are deliberately set to 350 CFM/ton to extract more moisture — this makes standard delta T charts/calculators inapplicable, since the slower airflow allows more heat absorption and reads higher than the standard chart expects
Field tip: If you're only checking delta T at the return grille and a random supply register, you're not really measuring delta T — you're measuring duct leakage plus delta T. Get the supply probe right at the air handler outlet.

A worksheet-style approach to finding subcooling by hand, useful for building the underlying visualization before relying on digital gauges that just spit out the number.

Definition
Subcooling is the number of degrees a liquid's temperature sits below its saturation (mixed liquid/vapor) temperature at the pressure it's measured at
Why it matters
Refrigerant must fully condense to liquid before the metering device to have a full column to feed. Too much subcooling ("flooded condenser") risks high head pressure and overcharge; too little ("starving condenser") risks inadequately feeding the metering device
Step 1 — actual liquid line temp
Clamp a temperature probe to the liquid line to get the actual liquid line temperature
Step 2 — liquid saturation temp
Attach a pressure manifold to the liquid line service port, then convert that pressure to a saturation temperature using a PT chart for the refrigerant in use
Step 3 — subtract
Subcool = liquid saturation temp − actual liquid line temp
Worked example (R-22)
115°F actual liquid line temp. 278 psig liquid line pressure corresponds to 125°F saturation temp. 125 − 115 = 10°F subcooling

Formula
Compression ratio = absolute discharge pressure ÷ absolute suction pressure. Absolute = gauge pressure + atmospheric (14.7 psi at sea level)
Worked example (R-22)
240 psig discharge + 14.7 = 254.7 psia. 75 psig suction + 14.7 = 89.7 psia. 254.7 ÷ 89.7 = 2.84:1
Typical AC range
2.3:1 to 3.5:1 — modern high-efficiency equipment trends toward the low end of this range
Medium-temp refrigeration (cooler)
3.0:1 to 5.5:1 typical (R-404A example)
Low-temp freezer (0°F to -10°F)
6.0:1 to 13.0:1 typical (R-404A example)

Compressor overheating, oil breakdown, high power consumption, low capacity
Ratio lower than designed
Possible indication of mechanical failure and poor compression — this is the low-CR compressor valve wear signature
Reducing high compression ratio

Field tip: A customer setting a thermostat unusually low, or a freezer working hard on a hot day, will show a high compression ratio that isn't a fault by itself — know the expected range for the application before treating it as a problem.

Follow the power, don't hopscotch around the unit. This is the same discipline whether it's a residential split system or a commercial RTU.

Safety first
LOTO whenever working in a commercial setting or out of sight of the disconnect. Never trust a disconnect blade — verify 0V between L1-L2, L1-Ground, and L2-Ground before touching anything
The schematic is your map
Trace the circuit mentally before your meter leads touch a terminal. If you don't know the sequence of operation, you're guessing, not troubleshooting
Diagnostic toolkit
True RMS multimeter (required for accurate readings on VFDs/ECM motors), amp clamp, fused jumper wires (3–5A, for safely bypassing controls to isolate circuits), megohmmeter for compressor winding-to-ground checks
Step 1 — high voltage
Measure at the disconnect, then line side of the contactor, then load side (T1/T2) if pulled in. 240V in but 200V out = pitted contacts creating resistance — replace the contactor
Step 2 — low voltage
If high voltage is present but the unit won't run, check R to C at the transformer secondary for 24V. Then check Y to C on a cooling call — 24V there means the thermostat is doing its job and the problem is downstream

A capacitor can test good cold on the bench and still fail once it's hot and under load — a bench MFD test alone isn't the full picture.

Capacitor load test
With the system running, measure amps on the start wire (common/HERM to compressor S terminal) and voltage across the capacitor (C to HERM)
Formula
µF = (Amps × 2652) ÷ Voltage. The constant 2652 is specific to 60Hz power
Tolerance
If the running result is outside ±6% or ±10% of the label rating (check which tolerance applies to that cap), replace it — even if it looks physically fine
Contactor voltage drop test
With the system running (contactor closed), measure voltage across each pole: L1 to T1, then L2 to T2
Contactor pass/fail
Good contacts read near 0V, ideally <0.1V. More than 0.5V across closed contacts means that resistance is generating heat and starving the compressor of voltage — replace it. A visual check alone (ants, light burns) isn't definitive
Field tip: If you find a failed capacitor, don't just swap it and move on — check condenser coil cleanliness and fan amp draw. Excess heat is a leading cause of capacitor failure, and a fresh cap on a dirty coil just buys time before the next one fails too.

When a compressor won't start, don't assume it's dead — distinguish the actual failure mode before condemning it.

Winding resistance check — single-phase (PSC)
Applies to single-phase compressors with Common/Run/Start terminals — most residential/light commercial reciprocating and single-phase scroll compressors. Measure resistance between C-R, C-S, and S-R. On a healthy compressor: R(C-R) + R(C-S) ≈ R(S-R)
Winding resistance check — three-phase (scroll / semi-hermetic)
Three-phase compressors don't have a common/run/start configuration — they have three separate windings. Measure across all three terminal pairs (T1-T2, T2-T3, T1-T3). On a healthy compressor, all three readings should be roughly equal to each other, not summed. A significantly unequal reading among the three points to a winding fault.
Ground check
Meter on the highest ohm scale (or a megger) from each terminal to clean copper ground. Any reading other than OL (infinite) indicates winding insulation breakdown
Locked rotor vs mechanical seize
Check LRA on the data plate. Pulls LRA and trips the breaker instantly → mechanically seized. Pulls significantly less than LRA but hums and trips on thermal overload → possible single-phasing or a loose connection, not a seized compressor
Field tip: Don't skip the ground check even when winding resistance looks fine — a compressor can have good winding-to-winding resistance and still be shorted to the shell.

The hardest calls are the ones that work when you arrive and fail when you leave. These patterns explain a lot of them.

Ghost voltage
Reading 40–80V on a disconnected wire is often induced voltage from adjacent live wires, not a real short. Switch the meter to Low-Z (Low Impedance) mode — if the voltage drops to zero, it's a ghost. If it holds, it's a live short
The rub-out
Check the low-voltage wire bundle where it enters the outdoor unit cabinet. Vibration rubs insulation raw against the refrigerant line or sheet metal, causing a short that only happens when the unit vibrates — won't show up sitting still
Loose neutrals
In the air handler, check the white neutral wire bundle. A loose neutral can cause bizarre voltage readings (like 60V showing on a 120V circuit) and erratic motor behavior that looks unrelated to the actual cause
Field tip: If a fault only shows up under vibration or load, don't just re-torque connections and call it fixed — flex the harness by hand while metering to try to reproduce the fault before you leave.

In a series circuit, total resistance is just the sum of every load's resistance. In parallel, it's trickier — and it goes down, not up, as you add more loads.

Formula
1÷Rt = 1÷R1 + 1÷R2 + 1÷R3 (add as many loads as are actually in parallel)
Worked example
R1=120Ω, R2=45Ω, R3=360Ω. 1÷120=0.0083, 1÷45=0.022, 1÷360=0.0028. Sum = 0.0331. 1÷0.0331 = 30.21Ω total — less than the lowest individual resistance in the circuit
Why total resistance drops
Each additional parallel path gives current another way through — more paths means less overall resistance, which by Ohm's law means higher total amperage
Real-world example
Compressor amps + condenser fan amps = total condenser circuit amps. This is parallel circuit behavior you already measure every day
Myth to drop
Electricity doesn't only take the path of least resistance — it takes all available paths. Every additional parallel path just lowers the overall resistance between the two points, which is why a breaker trips when you add another load to an already-loaded circuit
Field tip: If you need the resistance of just two loads in parallel, there's a shortcut: (R1 × R2) ÷ (R1 + R2) — same answer as the full formula, less arithmetic.
Full reference (diagrams, related series-circuit article): Parallel Circuit Resistance — HVAC School

A single-phase motor has three terminals (common, start, run) but only two windings. Common is just the shared point between them — which is why amperage is always measured on common, since that reads the combined current of both windings.

Why Ohm's law doesn't apply cleanly here
AC voltage/amperage isn't fixed (we measure RMS, not peak), and impedance in an inductive load isn't fixed either — it combines static winding resistance with inductive reactance that builds as the magnetic field expands/collapses and back EMF as the motor spins
Winding ohm readings are nearly meaningless
Static resistance readings are so low that meter inaccuracy becomes a real factor. The real resistance (impedance) only shows up once the motor is energized with AC and the magnetic fields are interacting
The run winding
Connected directly across the line. Current is high at start (low impedance) and drops as the motor spins up and impedance increases — this is where essentially ALL the true inrush current occurs
The start winding
Connected through the run capacitor, not directly across the line. Its current is capped by the capacitor's capacitance — it literally cannot draw more current than the capacitor allows, regardless of winding impedance
What this means for inrush readings
Nearly all inrush happens on the run winding, not the start winding. Start winding current doesn't rise until run winding current is already coming back down
Testing it yourself
Measure running amps on the start wire with a capacitor slightly oversized, slightly undersized, and removed entirely — you'll see higher amps, lower amps, then none. That's the capacitor acting as a current ceiling, not the winding
Field tip: Don't try to explain start winding amperage with static ohm readings and Ohm's law alone — the capacitor is doing the current-limiting, not the winding resistance.

"Short" gets used loosely to mean "anything electrically wrong." In technical terms, it refers to one of two specific faults.

Type 1 — two circuits connected in an undesigned manner
Example: a Y and G wire shorted together from abrasion in a thermostat cable — result: the condenser runs whenever the blower is energized
Type 2 — a no-load path between two points of differing charge
A traditional short to ground, a hot-to-common connection, or a connection between power legs without going through a load of appropriate resistance first
What both types cause
Something energizes when it shouldn't, or fuses/breakers trip/blow, damaging components
Short vs open
Open circuit = no path at all, power applied but nothing happens. Short circuit = fuses/breakers trip, or something energizes at the wrong time. Don't conflate the two when describing a fault
Finding a low-voltage short
A resettable fuse device works well. An ohmmeter is also commonly used, but process-of-elimination with the 24V circuit live is often faster for most techs than isolating with an ohmmeter
Field tip: Before calling something "shorted," identify which of the two technical definitions actually applies — it changes where you should be looking for the fault.

A general-purpose diagnostic approach: form a hypothesis, then test it one component or conductor at a time using process of elimination, rather than tearing the whole system apart at once.

First, before isolating
Visually check common failure points: rub-out points on low-voltage wires, the safeties, and control wire routing both inside and outside the condensing unit — especially anywhere wires cross refrigerant tubing. Check the thermostat wiring for exposed copper too. Don't just replace a blown fuse without looking — fuses don't fail open on their own
Low-voltage short isolation
Replace the blown fuse with a resettable fuse/breaker, thermostat off in all modes. Close the door switch first to confirm there's no continuous short in R. Then jumper each switch leg (G, Y, W, O, W2, Y2, etc.) to R one at a time — it's 24V, it won't hurt you — and watch for the one that blows the fuse or throws a spark
Narrowing further
Once you've found the offending leg, separate the two ends of that circuit (e.g. the wire going to the thermostat vs. the wire going to the condenser) and tap each one to R independently to localize the fault to a specific wire run or component
Shorted compressor — first move
Never just reset a tripped breaker on a condensing unit without investigating first. It tripped for a reason, and every reset risks a major arc — if the short is inside the compressor, that arc adds carbon and acid to the refrigerant
Visual inspection first
Full visual of all high-voltage wiring, terminals, the contactor, capacitor, crankcase heater, and the breaker itself before pulling the top to inspect compressor terminals. Wear gloves and safety glasses before handling compressor terminals or the plug — a terminal can blow out of the fusite under pressure
Ground resistance test
Measure resistance to ground from each compressor terminal. Manufacturers may still call a compressor good down to 0.5 megohms, while some meters flag "bad" as high as 20 megohms — know which standard applies before condemning a compressor on a borderline reading
Final confirmation
Tape/strap the compressor terminals so nothing can touch, reassemble, and reset the breaker. Doesn't trip and everything else runs normally — diagnosis confirmed. Trips again — back to the drawing board
Other uses
Same principle works for isolating system noises, or for confirming whether a communicating controller or its wire run is at fault by wiring the controller directly to the board with no wire run between them
Field tip: A fuse that keeps blowing and just gets replaced without investigation is a callback waiting to happen — fuses don't fail open on their own.

Ambient temperature rating refers to the air around the motor, not the motor's own shell temp or outdoor air temp — the motor often sits in a condenser air stream that's hotter than ambient.

Typical ambient ratings
Condensing fan motors: 60°C (140°F), 70°C (158°F), or 80°C (176°F). Blower motors: commonly 40°C (104°F)
Normal shell temp
Commonly 30–60°F warmer than outdoor temp during normal operation, depending on sun exposure, motor efficiency, and runtime
Common overheating pattern
Condensing fan motor overheats and trips on internal thermal overload during peak heat, which drives head pressure up until the compressor also trips — system may cool overnight and run fine when the tech arrives, causing a frustrating nuisance-intermittent callback
High condensing temperature
Dirty condenser or overcharge heats the air around the motor, which heats the motor
Direct sun
Radiant heat directly on the motor housing raises shell temp independent of ambient air
Voltage
Check motor voltage is in proper range while running under load, not just at idle
Capacitor
Both undersized and oversized capacitors can cause motor overheating — confirm correct sizing, don't just confirm it's "close enough"
Bearing issues
Failing bearings often show increased noise or side-to-side shaft play, but not always — don't rule bearings out just because the motor spins quietly
Field tip: A thermal camera is best used to compare a suspect motor against a similar unit running under similar conditions nearby, not as a single pass/fail reading — emissivity varies by surface and throws off absolute accuracy.

Three-phase AC motors are mechanically about as simple as a motor gets — the utility spins magnets to produce three phases 120° apart at 60Hz, and the motor's electromagnets follow that rotation.

Sizing
Motor must physically fit, match voltage, and be rated for the job. An oversized motor is occasionally acceptable if it fits and conductors/overloads are sized to match — an undersized motor is not
Overload protection
Many motors have internal overload protection. Others rely on an external overload, most commonly built into the starter (a contactor plus overload relay combined)
Rotation
Swapping any two phases reverses rotation. Blower motors and scroll compressors are the worst offenders for running backward when miswired — always confirm correct rotation before leaving a job. Note: if a blower is on a VFD and the compressor isn't, the VFD can correct blower rotation independently, which can mask a compressor wired in reverse
Voltage/current imbalance
A motor killer — increases winding heat and degrades performance. Check phase-to-phase (not phase-to-ground) with the motor running: average the three readings, then find the % deviation of the reading furthest from that average
Imbalance targets
DOE recommends ≤1% imbalance; other industry sources allow up to 4%. Keep it under 4% and work to correct anything over 1%. Current should track the same pattern as voltage since all three windings share equal resistance — investigate major current variation the same way
No run/start capacitors
Three-phase motors don't need run or start capacitors the way single-phase PSC motors do. They may use part-winding or other soft-start strategies, and may have power-factor-correction capacitors, but neither is the same thing as a single-phase run cap
Field tip: Always verify rotation direction on a 3-phase blower and compressor independently before leaving a job — a VFD-driven blower correcting its own rotation can hide a compressor that's still wired backward.

Builds on the imbalance measurement method under 3-phase motors basics — this card covers why imbalance happens and how to track it down.

Phase rotation importance varies
Doesn't matter for reciprocating compressors. Matters for condenser fans and blowers. Is absolutely critical for scroll and screw compressors — wrong rotation can damage them quickly
Confirming rotation
When replacing parts/equipment, keep phase connections the same as the original. Use a phase rotation indicator to confirm (clockwise is common but confirm for the specific equipment), or disconnect the compressor and verify blower rotation first before bringing the compressor online
VFD caveat
A motor on a VFD automatically corrects its own rotation regardless of incoming phase order — this makes a VFD-driven motor an unreliable way to test source phase rotation for other equipment on the same feed
Why imbalance happens
Electricians balance single-phase 120V and 208V loads across all three legs of a panel. Uneven loading — like a bunch of space heaters concentrated on one leg — leaves the lighter-loaded phase carrying less current and reading higher voltage, which is a real-world source of imbalance beyond the utility feed itself
Troubleshooting steps
Look for the obvious first: melted wires, loose terminals/lugs, undersized wire, pitted contacts, poor disconnect/fuse contact
Where to measure
Check voltage as close to the motor as possible first — that's the actual voltage the motor sees. Then work back toward the distribution point; a big voltage jump as you move toward the source pinpoints where the voltage drop is happening
Severity threshold
Over 4% imbalance, don't leave the motor running — the risk of damage and an expensive repair is real
Field tip: If several small single-phase loads and a large 3-phase RTU share the same panel, an uneven mix of on/off single-phase loads can itself be the source of an intermittent voltage imbalance — not just a utility-side issue.

General process-of-elimination approach for an ECM/X13 motor that isn't running. Most of the best practices and diagnostic tools for these come from Regal/Genteq's Dealer Toolbox resources and their TECInspect diagnostic tool.

1. Confirm power
Proper line voltage and 24V calls to the correct terminals in the equipment
2. Confirm control signal
Proper control signal reaching the motor from the 24V field wiring/board. Fan speed selection and programming vary by manufacturer — a dedicated tool like TECInspect helps verify this input signal is actually correct
3–4. Mechanical check
Power off, remove the blower housing, check for abnormal sounds and side-to-side bearing play. These motors have a permanent-magnet rotor, so they won't spin freely by hand like a standard motor — expect an "indexing" feel as you turn the shaft, not free rotation
5. Visual check
Look for signs of overheating, burned spots, or other visible damage
6. Isolate the motor
Remove the module from the motor and disconnect the plug connecting the motor to the module
7. Winding-to-winding (phase-to-phase)
Measure at the plug feeding the motor. Should read less than 20Ω and be nearly equal across all windings
8. Winding-to-ground
Measure from each winding to the casing/ground. Should read no less than 100k ohms
9. If motor and signal both check out
The module itself is the failed part and needs replacement

Oxygen in the air combines with copper to form surface oxide (light-to-dark brown discoloration). At brazing temperature a heavier black "cupric oxide" forms, which flakes off as scale that circulates through the system and can restrict flow through metering devices or reversing valve pilot capillary tubes.

Why it matters more now
POE oils (used with R-410A and other HFCs) have a solvent effect and can "scrub" tube walls, circulating oxide and scale that mineral oil (R-22 era) didn't
Procedure
Flow dry nitrogen through the tube during brazing to displace oxygen and prevent scale formation
Starting flow rate
2–3 cfh or 1.5–2 psi. Some techs set pressure until a slight flow is felt at the exit point on the back of the hand
Timing
Initiate flow before heating and continue until the part has cooled
Avoid excessive flow
High flow rate cools the tube (reducing brazing heat efficiency) and can reduce braze alloy penetration. A small hole in an end cap lets nitrogen escape without building pressure
Field tip: Test-braze on the bench and section the finished assembly to inspect for a clean inner-tube wall before relying on a given flow rate setting in the field.
Cut & clean
Cut tube square (tube cutter or hacksaw with a sawing fixture), ream/deburr inside and out. Joint surfaces must be clean and free of oil/grease/oxide — stainless wire brush or emery cloth, solvent for oil/grease, then wipe away any grit before assembly
Joint clearance
Hold .002"–.006" at brazing temperature (varies by alloy) — too loose or too tight both cause problems, this is not a "press-fit is fine" situation
Heating sequence
Heat the tube first, working the flame alternately around tube and fitting until both reach brazing temperature, before applying filler metal. On vertical joints, heat the tube first then the fitting, keeping the flame directed toward the fitting
Applying alloy
Only add filler metal once base metals are at brazing temperature. Feed into the joint, not the flame. Stop once the joint is filled — excess fillets waste material and don't improve joint quality
Flux behavior as a heat guide
Flux bubbles as it heats, then goes clear and quiet around 1100°F/593°C — that's your visual cue the joint is close to brazing temperature
Cleanup
Remove all flux residue after brazing — quench or wet-brush while still warm, emery cloth/wire brush if needed. Flux left in place is corrosive over time
Field tip: If the outside of the joint is hot but the alloy won't flow, the inside isn't actually up to temperature yet — heat the tube first so heat conducts into the fitting, not just the fitting surface.
Oxy-acetylene
Use a neutral-to-slightly-carburizing flame (well-defined inner cone). Avoid an oxidizing flame — it promotes oxide formation right when you're trying to prevent it
Air-acetylene
Set delivery pressure 14–15 psi if the tank has a delivery gauge. Open the torch valve about 3/4 turn for sufficient gas flow — don't try to throttle the flame size with the torch handle valve, change tip size instead
Sizing the flame to the joint
Air-fuel gives a wider heat pattern than oxy-acetylene, which helps larger tube diameters since heat wraps around the pipe. Oxy-acetylene gives the hottest, most focused heat zone
Field tip: If you're constantly adjusting the torch handle valve mid-joint to control heat, you're using the wrong tip size for that connection — swap tips instead of fighting the flame.
Copper-to-copper
Phos-copper alloys (Stay-Silv 5/15, Dynaflow) — self-fluxing on copper, no external flux needed for a straight copper joint
Copper-to-brass
Same phos-copper alloys work, but now require Stay-Silv white brazing flux — the self-fluxing property only applies to bare copper-to-copper
Ferrous metals (steel, stainless)
Never use phosphorus-bearing alloys on steel or other ferrous metals — it forms a brittle phosphide prone to cracking. Use a high-silver alloy (Safety-Silv 45/56) with white flux instead; black flux for stainless or long/localized heating cycles
Soldering (Stay-Brite) vs brazing
Stay-Brite silver-bearing solder (430–535°F) is a legitimate lower-temperature alternative to brazing on many joints — no oxide scale forms at that temp, so no nitrogen purge is needed. Trade-off: post-braze burst strength on a real brazed joint (1,800 PSI) is roughly half of pre-heat tube strength, while a properly soldered Stay-Brite joint keeps close to full tube strength since there's no annealing from high heat
Aluminum
Requires its own alloys (Al-Braze/Alcor), not standard copper alloys. Aluminum-to-dissimilar-metal joints can be subject to galvanic corrosion — flag this on repairs
Field tip: Getting phos-copper alloy on a steel fitting by habit is an easy mistake when you're used to copper-to-copper all day — check what you're actually joining before reaching for the rod.

Roughly 90% of joints that leak in service trace back to technique, not the alloy itself.

Alloy melts and fillets but won't flow into the joint
Outside of the joint is hot but the inside isn't at brazing temp yet — heat the tube first so heat conducts inward
Alloy balls up instead of wetting/flowing
Base metals aren't clean, or the joint was overheated and flux broke down (saturated with oxides, no longer active). Try a softer flame and/or heavier flux coating
Alloy flows away from the joint instead of into it
Fitting isn't up to temperature — redirect the flame toward the fitting, not just the tube
Filler metal cracks after solidifying
Dissimilar metal CTE mismatch (e.g. copper-to-steel, copper expands/contracts more), excessive joint clearance, or quenching too fast right after brazing — let it cool more before flushing flux residue
Repairing pinhole leaks
On phos-copper brazed joints, Blockade solder can often repair a pinhole without re-melting the original braze. On high-silver alloy joints, Stay-Brite solder works for pinhole repair. Never braze over a joint that was previously soldered with tin/lead — the low-melting solder residue interferes with proper alloying

The order: Compressor → Discharge line → Condenser → Liquid Line → Metering Device → Expansion Line → Evaporator → Suction line → back to Compressor. Everything else in this reference assumes you have this sequence memorized cold.

Compressor
The only mechanical component in a basic system. Creates the pressure differential — high side (discharge/condenser/liquid line) vs low side (suction/evaporator/expansion line). Compressing a gas raises its temperature; this is why discharge gas is hot
Condenser — three jobs, in order
1) Desuperheat (drop hot discharge gas down to condensing temp) 2) Condense (reject heat until it's fully liquid) 3) Subcool (drop the liquid's temp further below condensing temp). It must fully condense to liquid, or there's nothing for the metering device to meter
Metering device
Creates a pressure drop between the liquid line and evaporator. The pressure drop lowers saturation temp below the surrounding air temp, causing the refrigerant to start boiling (flashing) — a portion of it flashes to vapor immediately on the drop, before it even reaches the coil
Evaporator
Refrigerant boils at a constant temp (saturation) while absorbing heat. Once fully boiled to vapor, further heat absorption raises its temp above saturation — that rise is superheat
Suction line
Evaporator to compressor. Low-pressure superheated vapor — cool to the touch on A/C, cold on refrigeration
Discharge line
Compressor to condenser. High-temp, high-pressure superheated vapor
Liquid line
Condenser to metering device. High-pressure subcooled liquid, slightly above outdoor temp
Expansion line
Metering device to evaporator, when the two are physically separated (common on ductless mini-splits with the metering device in the outdoor unit). Full of mixed liquid/vapor flash gas
Superheat vs subcool vs saturation, in one line
Fully liquid = subcooled. Fully vapor = superheated. Actively changing state (boiling or condensing) = at saturation
Terminology note
The "condenser" commonly refers to the whole outdoor unit, which actually contains the condenser, compressor, and other parts — "condensing unit" or "outside unit" is the more precise term
Field tip: Heat and cooling are never created, only moved or changed in form — every diagnostic question ultimately comes back to "where is the heat coming from, and where is it going."

Heat rejection
Air-cooled: direct to outdoor air via coils/fans. Water-cooled: to a water loop, cooled by tower/fluid cooler
Efficiency
Air-cooled drops in hot weather. Water-cooled is higher efficiency, more stable, especially at scale
Maintenance
Air-cooled: coil cleaning, fan motors. Water-cooled: water treatment, basin cleaning, pump seals, tube cleaning
Common applications
Air-cooled: small-medium RTUs, splits, VRF. Water-cooled: large commercial/industrial, process cooling, data centers
Cooling method
DX: refrigerant absorbs heat directly at the evaporator coil. Chilled water: water cooled by a chiller, circulated to AHU coils
System type
DX: closed refrigerant circuit, self-contained per unit. Chilled water: central plant, multiple AHUs share one loop
Chilled water supply/return
Typically 42–45°F supply, 55–58°F return
Applications
DX: small-medium buildings, RTUs, splits, VRF. Chilled water: large commercial, industrial, hospital, campus
Key component
Heat pump has a reversing valve to switch heating/cooling. Straight AC has none — fixed refrigerant flow direction
Heating method
Heat pump extracts heat from outdoor air. Straight AC relies on a separate furnace or electric heat
Control wiring
Heat pump includes O/B terminal for reversing valve + aux heat. Straight AC has no O/B terminal
Defrost
Heat pumps need an automatic defrost cycle. Straight AC doesn't.
Control type
TXV: mechanical, sensing bulb pressure. Fixed orifice: fixed hole size, flow varies with pressure. EEV: electronic, stepper motor/solenoid via board feedback
Superheat control
TXV: maintains constant SH at evap outlet. Fixed orifice: no control, SH varies widely. EEV: precisely maintains target SH electronically
Responsiveness
TXV: moderate (mechanical delay). Fixed orifice: slow. EEV: fast and accurate
Typical applications
TXV: standard splits/commercial. Fixed orifice: budget/residential. EEV: VRF/VRV, chillers, modern inverter-driven systems
Scroll
Small-medium tonnage, high efficiency, very quiet. Common failures: scroll separation, flooded starts, liquid slugging damage
Reciprocating
Small-medium tonnage, older/budget units. More moving parts (valves, pistons, rods), more wear points, noisier
Screw
Medium-large tonnage, big RTUs/chillers/industrial. Excellent modulation via slide valve or VFD, but higher maintenance (oil management)
Typical target
350–450 CFM per ton (400 CFM/ton ideal). Example: 3-ton system → ~1,200 CFM target
Temperature split (ΔT)
Return − supply, typically 18–22°F for cooling
Heating airflow check
CFM = BTU output ÷ (1.08 × ΔT). Example: 80,000 BTU / (1.08 × 55°F) ≈ 1,360 CFM
Low airflow signs
Frozen coil, high head/low suction, hot spots, long run times
High airflow signs
Low temperature split, noisy ducts, poor humidity control
Field tip: Verify airflow before charging or adjusting refrigerant. Below 350 CFM/ton, fix airflow first — refrigerant readings will be misleading.
Normal ΔT — cooling
Residential 18–22°F, commercial 16–20°F
Normal ΔT — heating
Gas furnace 40–70°F, heat pump 20–30°F, hydronic coil 25–40°F
Low ΔT cooling
Possible undercharge, low airflow, or inefficient compressor. Low ΔT + high superheat = low charge
High ΔT cooling
Restricted airflow, dirty filters, or undersized return duct
Evaporator coil clues
Even frost = normal. Frost only at metering device = restriction. Ice across whole coil = low airflow
Field tip: Low ΔT + high amps = airflow issue. Low ΔT + low amps = refrigerant or compressor issue.

Flash gas is just another term for boiling. It's normal and required directly after the metering device and through the evaporator — that's how the coil absorbs heat via latent heat of vaporization. It's only a problem when it happens somewhere it shouldn't.

What causes flashing
A drop in pressure below the saturation point, a rise in temperature above it, or both. In the liquid line this usually means long/undersized lines, kinks, or a clogged filter drier — all of which drop pressure
Preventing it
Keep line lengths and tight bends to a minimum, insulate liquid line runs through hot spaces, keep refrigerant clean/dry with a properly sized drier
Subcooling as headroom
10°+ of subcooling gives the liquid line margin against pressure drop from long line runs — a system with proper subcooling normally won't flash in the liquid line under normal conditions
Bubbles in a sight glass ≠ automatically "flash gas"
Could genuinely be true flash gas in the liquid line. Could also just mean the refrigerant was never fully condensed to liquid in the first place (low charge) — in that case subcool will read 0° even at the condenser outlet
How to tell the difference
True liquid-line flash gas shows measurable subcooling at the condenser outlet, but you still see/hear/measure boiling refrigerant in the liquid line before the metering device
Field tip: Hissing or surging at the liquid line near the evaporator, or bubbles in the sight glass, means check subcool at the condenser first — that single reading tells you whether you're chasing a restriction or a charge problem.

Dry-bulb temperature (a regular thermometer reading) doesn't account for the actual heat content of air or its evaporative cooling potential — wet-bulb and enthalpy fill that gap.

What wet-bulb actually measures
The temperature a damp thermometer bulb reads in a 900 FPM air stream — exactly what a sling psychrometer measures. The bigger the gap between wet-bulb and dry-bulb (wet-bulb depression), the lower the RH and the greater the evaporative cooling effect available
At 100% RH
Dry-bulb, wet-bulb, and dew point are all the same — air is fully saturated, zero evaporative effect available
Enthalpy
Total heat content of the air, in BTUs per lb. Converting lbs of air to CFM lets you calculate a coil's actual heat-moving capacity, or BTU gain/loss over a duct run
Wet-bulb and enthalpy are linked
On a psychrometric chart, wet-bulb and enthalpy lines run together (at 14.7 PSIA) — a given wet-bulb temp corresponds to a specific enthalpy value. Reading this accurately matters: a difference of 28.4 vs 28.6 BTU/lb adds up fast multiplied across a large air mass
Field tip: This is the concept behind the cooling tower approach/wet-bulb numbers elsewhere in this reference — see "How a cooling tower actually works" under Cooling Towers for the applied version.

ACH describes how many times a room's total air volume is completely replaced per hour — a ventilation rate, not a heat gain/loss calculation. Don't confuse the two.

Formula
CFM × 60 minutes ÷ room volume (cubic feet) = ACH. A 10'x10'x10' room (1,000 cu ft) with 100 CFM balanced supply/return = 6,000 CFH ÷ 1,000 = 6 ACH
What it is NOT
Not the heat gain/loss of the space — that's a separate calculation. ACH is generally used as a design guideline/reference, not the actual basis for sizing airflow
Don't confuse with outdoor air requirements
Outdoor air ventilation requirements are a related but different consideration, governed by ASHRAE 62.1 and 62.2
ACH50
Used with a blower door to describe whole-structure "tightness" — the leak rate measured when the space is pulled to -50 pascals. Referenced in many codes as a tightness standard
Correct sizing order
Size room/zone airflow to heat gain/loss first (including outdoor air requirements), then let ventilation requirements dictate whether additional mechanical ventilation or fan-on time is needed — don't just hit an ACH number and assume the space is properly conditioned
Field tip: If a customer or design calls out an ACH number, confirm whether they mean whole-structure tightness (ACH50) or per-room ventilation rate — the term gets used loosely and means very different things in each context.

Always read the manufacturer's charging spec for the model first when possible. "What should my pressures be?" is not how you charge a system — charts and rules of thumb fill the gap only when factory data isn't available.

Which method for which metering device
Fixed orifice/piston systems: primarily superheat method. TXV/EEV systems: primarily subcool method. Always charge in cool mode when possible regardless of which mode you'll verify in later
Stop-and-investigate triggers
Suction saturation below 32°F with both indoor/outdoor temps above 68°F needs more diagnosis, even if SH/SC look correct. Liquid line pressure more than 30° saturation above outdoor temp (e.g. 440 PSI liquid on R-410A at 90°F) means stop and address high head before proceeding, regardless of SH/SC readings
Charging technique
Always purge hoses first. Charge in liquid phase (tank upside down), add slowly, watch the sight glass or use a liquid-preventing adapter to avoid flooding/slugging the compressor
Superheat charging
Monitor actual suction line temp vs low-side saturation temp, plus indoor/outdoor entering temps to calculate target SH from a manufacturer chart or calculator. Adding charge decreases superheat; recovering increases it
Subcool charging
Monitor liquid line temp vs high-side saturation temp. No entering-temp data needed. If no manufacturer spec is available, 10–12° subcool is a relatively safe residential default. Adding charge increases subcool; recovering decreases it
Approach method (Lennox TXV systems)
Approach = liquid line temp minus outdoor ambient (measured in shade, away from condenser discharge air). Get to at least 6° subcool before calculating approach. Remove charge to increase approach differential, add to decrease it
Heat pump heat mode charging
Switch hoses so suction gauge reads off common suction (between compressor and reversing valve). Rule of thumb only: discharge line temp 100° above outdoor ambient, or 20–25° suction saturation below outdoor ambient. Colder outside = lower suction pressure; hotter inside = higher head pressure, since the coil roles are reversed
Before touching charge
Full visual inspection first: filter, blower wheel, evaporator coil, condenser coil cleanliness, correct wiring/mode
Field tip: Test everything, not just the one number you're chasing — airflow, delta T, SH, SC, suction pressure, head pressure, amps, and incoming voltage all need to check out together before you call a charge correct.

Used to measure low pressures — under 1 PSI — since 1 PSI equals 27.71" WC. Originates from a literal water manometer: 1" WC is the force needed to raise a column of water 1 inch.

Gas pressure
Single-stage appliances commonly set to 3.5" WC natural gas, 11" WC propane — always confirm against manufacturer specs, combustion analysis, and meter clocking, this varies by unit
Calibration requirement
Manometers and Magnehelic gauges read relative to atmospheric pressure, not absolute — must be recalibrated/zeroed before each use to account for elevation and barometric pressure changes
Altitude consideration
Above 2,000 ft elevation, follow manufacturer guidance on gas valve adjustment and possible orifice size changes — lower atmospheric pressure affects gas delivery
Static pressure
Pressure exerted in all directions in a contained space — not a directional force. Measure negative return-side pressure (before the blower, after the filter when possible) and positive supply-side pressure (after the blower), then combine for total external static
Worked example
Return static -0.3" WC + supply static +0.2" WC = 0.5" WC total external static
Two-port gauges
Most manometers and all Magnehelics have two ports to read differential pressure in one measurement — also useful for testing furnace air pressure switch make/break points
Field tip: If your manometer reading looks off, check calibration/zero before questioning the system — especially after a change in elevation between jobs or a big weather swing.

The same amount of moisture in the air can read as a very different relative humidity depending on temperature — understanding why explains a lot of "confusing" RH readings in the field, including a supply air RH that reads near 100% on a properly dehumidifying system.

Relative humidity (RH)
The ratio of moisture actually in the air to the maximum moisture the air could hold at that temperature. NOT the total amount of moisture present
Absolute humidity
The true total amount of moisture in the air, measured in grains. Read off a psychrometric chart using dry-bulb temp and RH. Useful for exact moisture content, but doesn't tell you how close the air is to its dew point the way RH does
Why warmer air reads lower RH for the same moisture
Air can hold more moisture when it's warmer and less when it's colder — the same grains of moisture read as a much higher RH in cold air than in warm air, since cold air's maximum capacity is lower
Why supply air RH often reads near 100%
Cooling air at the coil lowers how much moisture it can hold, so moisture condenses out and drains away. The remaining air leaving the coil is close to fully saturated (near 100% RH) even though its total moisture content (grains) is much lower than the return air — this is normal, not a sign of poor dehumidification
Why space RH drops well below supply RH
As the cold, near-saturated supply air moves through ductwork and mixes with the warmer space air, it reheats. Warmer air can hold more moisture, so RH drops significantly on the way to the space — without any additional moisture being removed
Dehumidifiers use the same principle
An evaporator and condensing coil in one box — the evaporator pulls moisture out while cooling the air, and the condensing coil reheats it, trading some sensible cooling capacity for latent (moisture) capacity
Target indoor RH
30–60% is the generally recommended range for IAQ purposes
Best-practice dehumidification airflow
Longer runtimes and slower fan speeds give the coil more contact time with the air, improving moisture removal
Field tip: A high RH reading right at the supply register is not by itself evidence of a dehumidification problem — check total moisture removal (grains, or space RH) before condemning the coil or charge.

Companion to the wet-bulb & enthalpy card elsewhere in this tab — this one covers where the terms come from and how they're actually used on a charging chart or service call.

Where the terms come from
A bare mercury thermometer bulb gives the dry-bulb (sensible-only) reading. Wrap the bulb in a wet cloth and expose it to airflow, and evaporative cooling from the cloth pulls the reading down — that's the wet-bulb temperature, which accounts for latent heat/humidity
Why superheat charging charts need both
Indoor wet-bulb temp is taken at the evaporator coil, since it captures the full heat (sensible + latent) moving over the coil — latent BTUs take a lot more energy to move than sensible ones. Outdoor dry-bulb temp is taken at the condenser. Return DB and WB are also needed to find target delta T
When WB and DB match
Only at 100% RH (the dew point) — anywhere below full saturation, the two readings will differ at the same location
Tools
Modern psychrometric probes replace the older sling psychrometer. Cheaper tools are more prone to sensor inaccuracy — paying for quality matters more here than on some other tools
Tool maintenance
Psychrometric sensors get dirty with regular use and batteries need replacing. Keep more than one probe and calibrate them against each other periodically to catch drift before it affects a diagnosis
Field tip: If two psychrometric probes on the truck start disagreeing with each other, that's your cue to calibrate — don't assume the newer or more expensive one is automatically the accurate one.

Companion to the Inches of Water Column card elsewhere in this tab — this one covers the tools and technique for actually taking a static pressure reading.

What static pressure is
Force exerted in all directions within a contained fluid (the air) — not the directional force of moving air, which is velocity pressure. Measured in inches of water column, the pressure needed to displace one inch of water in a water manometer
Magnehelic
A brand name for a Dwyer analog differential pressure gauge in various scales. Many techs now use a digital differential manometer instead (e.g. Fieldpiece SDMN5), making a separate Magnehelic largely unnecessary
Setup
Zero the tool to room pressure first (level a Magnehelic before zeroing). Negative probe goes in the return side after the filter but before the blower, kept away from the blower itself. Positive probe goes in the supply duct. Insert probes straight and square
Static tip vs pitot tube
A static pressure tip (pointed opposite the airflow direction) prevents velocity pressure/air currents from skewing the reading. A pitot tube measures velocity or total pressure (velocity + static), not static alone, and looks different — open end, two connection points. Don't confuse the two
Target total external static (TESP)
TESP = return + supply. Generally 0.5" or less is the target on residential/light commercial systems; design goal is 0.4"–0.6" total. 0.8"+ starts causing trouble on most newer residential systems, though motor design varies by equipment
Isolating high static
Read return and supply separately (move the probe/hose between them) to find which side is driving the problem
Field tip: If total static is high, don't guess which side is the culprit — read return and supply separately. It takes seconds and tells you exactly where to focus.
