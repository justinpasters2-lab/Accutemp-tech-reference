The referenced Mitsubishi SM-series documentation lists a 50% minimum combination ratio (sum of connected indoor unit capacity vs outdoor unit rated capacity) for that platform. Falling below the documented minimum for a given outdoor unit causes compressor modulation failures that look like a charge problem but aren't.

Formula
Combination ratio = total connected IDU capacity ÷ ODU rated capacity × 100
Minimum ratio (per referenced SM-series documentation)
50% — confirm this figure against the current spec sheet for the exact outdoor unit before relying on it
Symptom if violated
Compressor won't modulate down properly, short cycling, capacity control faults
Field tip: Don't chase charge or SH/SC on an undersized combination ratio system — fix the ratio (add IDUs or downsize the ODU) first, then re-evaluate.

Mitsubishi factory procedure for outdoor unit compressor/accumulator replacement. Licensed HVAC professionals only — refer to full manufacturer instructions before attempting.

1. Recover charge
Both high and low side service ports. Current models may lack L-shaped adapters — use valve fittings instead. Heat may be needed at accumulator/compressor to fully recover charge; never use open flame.
2–5. Access
Remove exterior panels, compressor compartment access cover, and top cover (may require removing blanking cover, transformer box, and lowering the electrical panel depending on model)
6–7. De-braze & unmount
De-braze suction and discharge line joints closest to the compressor (or accumulator joints if replacing that). Remove compressor mounting bolt nuts and anti-vibration bracket — one nut is normally missing by design. Remove crankcase heater/thermistor if equipped.
8–10. Oil check
Pour oil from suction pipe into a graduated container. Less than 1 liter (32 oz) or any foreign matter → consult Mitsubishi rep. Acid-test the oil to determine correct filter core if installing a PAC-SPRFCS filter kit.
11–14. Install & braze
Install new compressor/accumulator, filter kit if used, braze all joints while purging with nitrogen at 0.5–2 PSI, reinstall mounting hardware and crankcase heater
15–20. Pressure test & evacuate
Pressurize to 600 PSI with nitrogen and leak check. Evacuate to 4000 microns, break with nitrogen, re-evacuate to 1500 then 500 microns. Hold 500 microns for 30 min pump time, then 1 hour standing.
21. Recharge
Weigh in charge as liquid to the high side only, never the low side. New accumulator requires oil added to the suction line before the accumulator via a two-port oil charge tank.
22–23. Reset compressor hours
Read/reset via dip switches on the control board — switch sequence differs between J-generation and K/L-generation units. Confirm hours read back to zero after reset.
Field tip: Note the missing mounting nut on step 7 is normal, not a prior tech's mistake — don't waste time hunting for a 4th nut that was never there.
Unit-specific — confirm with current manufacturer service manual for the exact model.

Confirm airflow and voltage independently before any of this points at charge.

Amps at/above RLA
Hot discharge + liquid line at cond outlet → high head. Check condenser fan amps/airflow before blaming charge.
Amps below RLA
Warm-to-cool suction, reduced discharge temp → undercharge or worn valves. Sharp cold spot on liquid line = restriction instead.
Low ΔT (below ~18°F)
Confirm blower amps vs rated tap first. Excess airflow (wrong tap) is an air problem — fix airflow before touching charge.
High ΔT (above ~24°F)
Check blower amps vs tap — low airflow (dirty filter/coil, closed damper) starves evap and mimics undercharge.
Frost migrating past coil onto suction line
Overfeeding — bad TXV, or straight overcharge on a piston system.

SH is the controlled variable on a TXV — it holds unless charge is way off. SC is the primary charge indicator.

Overcharge
No-gauge: amps at/above RLA, liquid+discharge hot, frost normal. Confirm: head high, SC high, SH normal-to-low.
Undercharge
No-gauge: amps below RLA, suction warm, liquid line uniformly warm. Confirm: both pressures low, SH high, SC low.
Liquid line restriction
No-gauge: amps starved, sharp cold spot at drier/fitting. Confirm: sharp pressure/temp drop across restriction, SH high.
Evap overfeeding / failing TXV
No-gauge: frost migrating onto suction line, low ΔT. Confirm: SH low/near zero, suction pressure high for load.

No modulation here — SH is the primary charge indicator, SC follows charge directly.

Overcharge
No-gauge: amps at/above RLA, liquid+discharge hot. Confirm: head high, SH low, SC high.
Undercharge
No-gauge: amps below RLA, liquid line warm, no cold spot. Confirm: both pressures low, SH high, SC low.
Restriction
No-gauge: amps below RLA, localized frost at drier, sight glass flashing. Confirm: SH high, SC low, sharp temp drop, flashing sight glass.
Compressor valve wear
No-gauge: amps below RLA despite normal charge signs. Confirm: suction high for load, head low for ambient, SH high/SC low despite adequate charge. Low compression ratio is the tell.
Non-condensables
No-gauge: amps at/above RLA, head high for ambient, all lines proportionally hot. Confirm: condenser sat temp reads higher than SC/ambient can explain.
Dirty/restricted condenser airflow
No-gauge: head high for ambient but fan amps/RPM confirmed correct → points to coil face restriction, not the fan.
Field tip: If condenser airflow independently confirms good and the ambient-to-sat-temp delta is still abnormal, suspect non-condensables over a dirty coil.

Don't chase a component until the SH/SC pattern matches one bucket cleanly. If it doesn't fit any bucket, suspect a compound fault — undercharge stacked with a restriction, or non-condensables masking an actual overcharge.

Protocol
Resolve the most obvious candidate first, document readings, then re-check the remaining axis. Don't correct two variables at once.
Document
Get customer acknowledgment on any deferred safety item before leaving site if full remediation isn't happening same visit.
Target numbers (ballpark only)
Evap ΔT 18–22°F · TXV superheat (evaporator, coil outlet) 8–12°F · Air-cooled SC 10–15°F · Compressor amps ±10% RLA

CLO stands for Current-Sensing Lock Out (also called Compressor Lock Out). It prevents restart of the compressor/unit after a safety trip until power is momentarily interrupted to the CLO. Applies to Carrier 38/48/50 series units.

Normal operating condition
18–30 VAC at Terminals 1 & 2, with 2.5A through the current sensor loop — relay stays de-energized
Trip condition
Current drops to 0.5A → relay energizes within 1 second, opens N.C. contact between 2 & 3, closes N.C. contact between 2 & X (alarm signal)
What trips it
HPS (high pressure switch), LPS (low pressure switch), or FPT (freeze protection thermostat) opening in the C1 compressor contactor circuit
Field tip: A compressor that won't restart after a nuisance trip, with no obvious fault present, is often just waiting on the CLO to be reset by cycling power — check the CLO before condemning the compressor or contactor.
Unit-specific — confirm with current manufacturer service manual for the exact model.
Full reference (Carrier TIP05-002, wiring diagram): CLO operation procedure PDF
Purpose
Brings in cool outdoor air to reduce/eliminate compressor run time (free cooling), most active 45–65°F outdoor
Control types
Dry-bulb (compares OA temp to setpoint) or enthalpy (compares total heat — more accurate, prevents humid OA use)
Mixed air target
Typically 55°F
Actuator signal
2–10VDC or 4–20mA modulating
Common failures
Stuck/seized dampers, broken actuator linkage, failed OA or mixed-air sensor, miswired control board
Field tip: If the economizer doesn't open in cool weather, check actuator voltage and sensor resistance first — 90% of failures are sensor or linkage-related, not the board.

Systematic fuse-isolation method for a resettable low-voltage fuse that keeps tripping.

1. Verify power source
Check transformer secondary (24–28 VAC). No 24V → check primary, repair/replace transformer.
2. Remove all thermostat wires
Reset fuse, power up. Trips → short is inside the unit (go to step 3). Holds → short is in the stat/cable, reconnect one wire at a time.
3. Isolate components one at a time
Disconnect (in order): gas valve, high-limit/aux safeties, economizer/OA damper harness, blower relay coil, condenser contactor coil. Reset fuse before each test. Fuse holds after disconnecting X → X is shorting.
4. Still trips with everything disconnected
Inspect harness for rubbed wires on blower deck, pinched wires near gas valve bracket, wires touching heat exchanger, or burned transformer secondary touching chassis.
5. Confirm
Reconnect components one at a time until fuse trips again — the last one reconnected is the fault.

Unloaders reduce compressor capacity by isolating a cylinder bank from the suction manifold, letting the unloaded cylinders spin without pumping refrigerant. Two control types on Carrier reciprocating compressors: electric (remote signal to a solenoid) and pressure-operated (driven by suction/discharge differential).

Electric — loaded (de-energized)
Valve passageways aligned for loaded conditions. No manual adjustment required.
Electric — unloaded (energized)
Solenoid opens the capacity control valve port, venting discharge gas from behind the unloader piston back to suction — isolates that cylinder bank, no compression
Pressure-operated — loaded
Suction pressure overcomes control set point spring → poppet valve closes → discharge gas builds against unloader piston → bank runs fully loaded
Pressure-operated — unloaded
Suction pressure drops below set point → diaphragm snaps, opens poppet → discharge gas vents to suction side → cylinder bank isolated
Control set point range
0–86 psig, adjustable. Each full turn CCW on the adjustment nut lowers load-up point ~7.2 psi
Pressure differential range
6–16 psi between load-up and unload points. Each full turn CW on the adjustment screw increases differential ~0.8 psi
Startup behavior
On pressure-operated units, controlled cylinders don't load until suction/discharge differential reaches approx 25 psig
Field check
Solenoid should give an audible click when actuated (electric type). No click with 24V present — suspect solenoid coil.
Field tip: Cylinder head assemblies include the capacity control valve — for electrically actuated heads, the solenoid coil is purchased separately. Confirm valve plate gasket thickness (0.32" for 06ER/EY refrigeration models, 0.07" for all others) before reassembly.
Unit-specific — confirm with current manufacturer service manual for the exact model.
Full reference (cutaway diagrams, torque specs, adjustment curves): Compressor unloaders instruction sheet PDF
Purpose
Removes ice from outdoor coil during heating mode to maintain airflow/heat transfer
Defrost types
Time/temperature (preset intervals) vs demand defrost (coil sensor + ambient delta — more efficient, common on Rheem/Trane/Carrier)
Defrost initiation
Coil sensor ~30°F with outdoor fan running in heating. Compressor keeps running, outdoor fan shuts off, reversing valve shifts to cooling
Defrost termination
Coil temp rises to ~55°F or max time (typically 10 min) expires. Board resets to heating, fan restarts, valve returns to heat
Sensor resistance check
Typically 10k–30kΩ at 32°F — replace sensor if out of range
Common issues
Sensor out of range/open circuit, failed reversing valve, board not sending defrost command, fan relay stuck on
Field tip: Never defrosts → check coil sensor and board voltage output. Stays in defrost → termination sensor or relay stuck.

Checks a single-phase compressor motor for winding-to-winding shorts (partially shorted windings) after the motor has already been checked for grounded, open, or shorted windings.

Rule of thumb
Resistance of start winding (C to S) should equal 3 to 6 times the resistance of run winding (C to R)
Example — good compressor
Start = 3.0Ω, Run = 0.5Ω → 3.0/0.5 = 6 → good
Example — partially shorted
Start = 1.25Ω, Run = 0.5Ω → 1.25/0.5 = 2.5 → partially shorted (ratio under 3)
Safety
Compressor terminals must not be disturbed while system is pressurized. Do not remove/connect wiring at the compressor unless system pressure is reduced to 0 psig.
Field tip: Use the R×1 ohm scale and zero the meter first — these are extremely low resistance values, and a good compressor can get misjudged as bad with an inaccurate meter setup.
Types
Air-cooled (condenser fans reject to ambient, easier install/maintain) vs water-cooled (condenser water + tower, more efficient for large systems)
Typical chilled water temps
Leaving 42–45°F, entering 52–55°F, ΔT typically 8–12°F
Condenser water temps (water-cooled)
In/out typically 85°F / 95°F
R410A pressures (air-cooled example)
Suction 115–130 psi, discharge 350–400 psi (varies with ambient)
R134a pressures (water-cooled example)
Suction 65–75 psi, discharge 120–140 psi typical at 44°F evap / 95°F cond
Water flow requirements
Chilled water 2.4 GPM/ton, condenser water 3.0 GPM/ton
Evaporator approach
Leaving water temp − evaporating temp, typically 8–12°F
Condenser approach
Condensing temp − leaving condenser water temp, typically 10–15°F. High approach = fouled tubes or low flow
Field tip: If chilled water temps fluctuate or the unit short cycles, check water flow and ΔT before adjusting refrigerant charge — roughly 80% of chiller faults are water-side, not refrigerant-side.
Low water cut-off (LWCO)
Prevents firing when water level is too low. Test weekly, clean float chamber annually.
Pressure relief valve
Releases excess pressure. Test annually by lifting lever, replace every 5 years or if leaking.
High limit / aquastat
Shuts off burner if water temp exceeds setpoint (typ. 200–210°F). Ensure manual reset works.
Flame safeguard
Monitors pilot/main flame, locks out on flame failure. Check signal, typically >2µA or >2V DC.
Rollout switch
Detects flame/heat escaping combustion chamber. Manually resettable, test for continuity.
Flow switch
Ensures water flow before burner operation. Test for continuity only when pump is running.
Gas pressure switches (hi/lo)
Monitor inlet/manifold gas pressure. Test with manometer, adjust only per manufacturer spec.
Condensate float switch
Prevents operation if condensate line backs up. Test by lifting float or adding water.
Field tip: Never bypass or jumper a safety for diagnostics without supervision — a failed safety can lead to severe equipment damage or unsafe operation.
Sequence of operation
Call for heat → safety interlocks prove → combustion fan pre-purges → ignition trial → gas valve opens → flame proven → modulates to setpoint

O₂ 3–6%, CO₂ 7–9%, CO <100 ppm steady-state, stack temp 325–500°F

O₂ 4–7%, CO₂ 8–10.5%, CO <80 ppm steady-state, stack temp 100–140°F. Keep return water ≤130°F for max efficiency

Gas pressure — manifold
Natural gas ~3.5" W.C., propane ~10" W.C.
Efficiency targets
Non-condensing 80–86%, condensing 90–96%
Field tip: When tuning, verify air intake, gas pressure, and venting first — combustion issues are usually airflow or vent-related, not gas valve faults. Adjust O₂ in 0.1–0.2% steps and recheck CO after every change.

Symptom-to-cause matrix for combustion analyzer readings. Always follow manufacturer's specifications for the specific burner.

Low O₂ / high CO
Insufficient combustion air (adjust air band), burner over-firing (adjust fuel), or low stack draft (adjust/install barometric control, check heat exchanger, check chimney/vent sizing)
High O₂
Excess combustion air (adjust air band), burner under-firing (adjust fuel), excess draft (adjust/install barometric control), or loose ports/doors/missing gasket (check & repair)
Unstable O₂ / CO readings
Changing weather/atmospheric conditions (check draft/barometric control), cracked heat exchanger (check/replace), excess draft, or loose ports/doors/missing gasket
High stack temp
Insufficient airflow across heat exchanger — increase blower speed, increase supply/return ducting, check for dirty blower/filter/coils
Low stack temp
Burner under-firing (adjust fuel), excess air flowing past heat exchanger (check temp rise vs manufacturer spec), or high fan speed (decrease fan speed/blower to reduce airflow)
Low stack draft (fuel oil, < -.04" W.C.)
Wrong sized venting/chimney, blocked vent, low combustion air, chimney/vent leak, or barometric control needs adjustment
High stack draft (fuel oil, > -.06" W.C.)
Barometric control needs adjustment/install, or wrong sized venting/chimney
Cracked heat exchanger check
Place probe in stack, watch O₂ and excess air readings as blower turns on — a substantial change indicates a cracked heat exchanger (on oil systems, possibly a missing gasket or loose clean-out port instead)
Field tip: Run this matrix against actual analyzer readings before touching gas pressure or air band — a symptom like high stack temp is often airflow-side, not a combustion tuning issue.

Flue gas temp
Condensing ~100–140°F vs non-condensing ~300–450°F
Return water temp requirement
Condensing must stay below ~130°F (ideal 110–120°F) or it operates like non-condensing and loses efficiency
Heat exchanger material
Condensing: stainless steel/aluminum (acid-resistant). Non-condensing: copper/cast iron/steel
Venting
Condensing: PVC/CPVC/polypropylene, sealed combustion. Non-condensing: metal Category I vent with draft hood
Common field mistake
Piping a condensing boiler to a high-temp loop without a mixing valve — no condensation occurs, efficiency is lost
Valve types
Single-stage (on/off), two-stage (low/high), modulating (0–10VDC or PWM signal)
Sequence
Thermostat calls → inducer starts, proving switch closes → ignitor energizes → gas valve opens → flame sensor proves flame
Valve coil test
Resistance typically 30–50Ω. 24V present but no click → replace valve. No 24V → trace back through safeties.
Combustion safety checks
Inducer draft pressure -0.4" to -0.8" W.C., steady blue flame core, clean burners
Common failures
Coil open/shorted, regulator drift, valve stuck closed, contaminated seat (oil/debris)
Field tip: Ignition starts but no flame → check for 24V at the gas valve coil. Voltage present but no gas → replace the valve. No voltage → trace back through pressure and rollout safeties.
Purpose
Absorbs expansion of heated water in a closed loop to prevent overpressure
Types
Diaphragm/bladder (most common) vs compression (no bladder, needs frequent air charge checks)
Location
At the point of no pressure change (PONPC), typically on the suction side near the fill point
Pre-charge pressure
Factory ~12 psi, adjust to system static fill pressure — rule of thumb: 1 psi per 2.31 ft of system height
Failure symptoms
Relief valve leaking, pressure fluctuating, noisy pump cavitation, tank heavy and full of water (no air charge)
Field tip: Always test air charge cold, with the system isolated and drained. A waterlogged or undercharged tank is one of the most common causes of relief valve discharge.
Flow formula
GPM = BTU ÷ (500 × ΔT). Example: 60,000 BTU coil at 20°F ΔT = 6 GPM
ΔT reference
Heating coil 15–25°F, cooling coil 8–12°F. Low ΔT = over-pumping/low load, high ΔT = low flow/plugged coil
Verification steps
Record entering/leaving temps → measure ΔP across circuit setter → use chart to confirm GPM → adjust valve → recheck ΔT
Air binding
Entrapped air reduces/blocks flow at high points. Purge and check auto air vents — air-bound coils show low ΔP and erratic ΔT
Field tip: Balance main loops first, then branches. A 1°F sensor error causes a 5%+ error in GPM calculation — use calibrated sensors.
Primary loop
Main loop connected directly to boiler/chiller. Constant flow, sized for the heat source. Protects it from flow starvation.
Secondary loop
Connected via closely spaced tees, variable flow via zone valves/VFD pumps to meet zone load.
Decoupling
Closely spaced tees or a hydraulic separator decouple the loops' pressure while allowing temperature transfer between them.
Common problems
Incorrect tee spacing, undersized pump, or reverse flow causing temperature fluctuations on the primary side

Identify assembly generation first: first-gen burners use 1/4x20 studs with no stud under the air arms; second-gen use 3/8 studs with an extra stud under each air arm.

Burner cleaning
90 psi continuous air, tight circular pattern from the inside out, unit upside down. 30–60 min typical. Pay special attention to the flame sensor 2 shield inside the burner.
Reassembly
Loctite 567 around each stud above/below each gasket. 1/4x20 studs torque to 6–8 ft-lb, 3/8 studs to 25 ft-lb. Watch for gasket bulging at either torque.
Leak check
Remove 4-wire PWM plugs from the fans, use Windex-type spray to generate pressure and check all gaskets. Run to operating temp, cool down, retorque all bolts a second time.
Flame pattern check
Two techs: one up top, one at the control. Gas valve 1&2 high: flame blue, ¾"–1½" long, well defined. Gas valve 1&2 low: flame mostly orange, tight to burner.
Flame signal target
Both flame signals 9.0 microamps or better — 11–13 is a good range
Combustion targets
CO 20ppm or less at low fire, 80ppm or less at high fire
Field tip: Do not adjust combustion to book numbers if operational testing is already complete and you meet the final criteria (no leaks, no noise, good flame signals, good flame patterns).

Head (ft)
Resistance the pump must overcome. 1 ft of head ≈ 0.433 psi (30 ft head = 13 psi)
Reading a curve
As flow increases, head decreases — curve slopes downward. Operating point = where pump curve meets system curve
Sizing example
Coil needs 10 GPM at 30 ft head — select a pump whose curve passes through that point near mid-range
Oversized pump
Too much flow → noise, erosion, wasted energy. Verify by measuring ΔP vs expected TDH; may need impeller trim or VFD
Undersized pump
Can't reach design flow → poor heating/cooling. Check voltage, rotation, or clogging before assuming replacement
Field verification
Use suction/discharge gauges to measure differential pressure across the pump, convert psi to feet of head (psi × 2.31)
Field tip: Ideal operation is near the middle of the pump curve, where it runs efficiently and quietly.
Open loop
Tower water exposed directly to atmosphere. Open-cell/crossflow/counterflow tower. Prone to contamination, scaling, algae.
Closed loop
Sealed system, no air contact, heat exchanged through a coil. Closed-circuit fluid cooler. Cleaner, less oxygen corrosion.
Visual tell
Spray nozzles/fill media visible = open loop. Finned coils with fan above sealed coil section = closed loop.
Field tip: If water contacts air directly, it's open loop. If water/glycol is isolated inside coils or piping, it's closed loop.
Startup checklist
Verify basin cleaned/filled → check float valve → confirm pump prime → inspect belts/fan/bearings → energize fan and check rotation
Winterization
Drain basin/piping → blow out supply/return lines → open all drains → clean fill/strainers → disable makeup water. Leave drains open through winter.
Approach temperature
Leaving water temp − ambient wet bulb temp. Typical 5–10°F. High approach = fouled fill, poor airflow, or reduced water flow
Cooling range (ΔT)
Entering − leaving water temp. Typical 10–15°F. Low ΔT = low load/excess flow. High ΔT = low flow/fouled coil
Water treatment
Monitor conductivity, pH 7.0–9.0, biocide levels. Poor water quality reduces heat transfer and damages components
Field tip: If approach temperature rises above 10°F, inspect fill, basin, and fan system. Low airflow = dirty blades or fan motor. Low water flow = clogged strainers or blocked nozzles.
Control method
CV: cycling compressors or modulating coil valves. VAV: adjusting zone damper position to vary airflow
Airflow
CV: constant at all times. VAV: modulated continuously by VAV boxes while supply temp stays relatively constant
Energy efficiency
CV: less efficient, fans run full speed at partial load. VAV: highly efficient, fan speed reduces via VFD
Typical equipment
CV: RTUs, small AHUs, simple systems. VAV: large AHUs, multi-zone buildings
Controls/BAS
CV: usually thermostat-based, minimal BAS. VAV: fully BAS-integrated with static pressure reset, scheduling, trending

Subcooling — where measured
Liquid line leaving the condenser (high side)
Evaporator superheat — where measured
Suction line at or near the evaporator coil outlet (low side)
Compressor / total superheat — where measured
Suction line at the compressor suction service valve — includes any heat gain along the suction line beyond the evaporator
SC normal range
TXV system 8–15°F typical, piston system 10–20°F
Evaporator SH normal range (coil outlet)
TXV system 8–12°F typical, piston system 15–25°F
Compressor / total SH normal range
17–22°F typical, measured at compressor suction
High SC means
Overcharge, restricted liquid line, dirty condenser, or low airflow
Low SC means
Undercharge, weak compressor, low head pressure, or undersized metering device
High evaporator SH means
Undercharge, low evaporator load, or restriction before the metering device
Low evaporator SH means
Overcharge, TXV stuck open, or poor airflow
Field tip: The 8–12°F figure is evaporator SH, taken right at the coil outlet, and reflects evaporator performance and metering device operation. The 17–22°F figure is compressor/total SH, taken at the compressor suction valve, and includes suction line superheat gain. The two are not interchangeable — a normal evaporator SH alongside an abnormally high compressor SH points to the suction line (undersized, poorly insulated, excess length) rather than the metering device. SC checks the condenser and charge on the high side; SH checks the evaporator and metering device on the low side. If both SC and evaporator SH are low, the system is undercharged or underloaded; if both are high, suspect restriction or non-condensables.

Every schematic has five basic components: a power supply, a path for the power, a load, a switch/component that interrupts power to the load, and a legend explaining the symbols used.

Ladder / schematic diagram
Most common type — symbols on rungs like a ladder, shows how the circuit functions rather than how it looks
Line diagram
Drawings resemble the actual components rather than symbols
Installation diagram
Used by installing contractors — shows terminal board connections only, rarely internal wiring
Reading junctions
A dot (solid black circle) = wires connected. No dot at a crossing = wires cross without connecting. Some manufacturers use loops/jumps for crossovers instead — check the legend.
Configuration — side by side
Loads/motors on the right ("load side"), switches/controllers on the left ("line side")
Configuration — up and down
High-voltage section on top, low-voltage on bottom. The transformer is usually the dividing line.
Line locators
Numbers along the left edge mark line location. Underlined number = normally closed contact. Non-underlined = normally open.
Common legend abbreviations
C1 = compressor contactor coil · CAP = run capacitor · CC HTR = crankcase heater · COMP = compressor · HPS/LPS = high/low pressure switch · IFR/IFM = indoor fan relay/motor · TRSF = transformer
Field tip: Always read the notes and legend first before tracing a schematic — not all manufacturers use the same conventions for dots, crossovers, or line weights.

Why it matters
Too little tension = slippage, excess heat, premature belt/pulley wear. Too much = excess stress on belts, bearings, shafts
Feel method accuracy
Only 1% of experienced techs tension a belt correctly by feel in controlled testing — most land at 7–50% of correct tension
Force-deflection method
Measure span length P. Apply force F perpendicular to the span, enough to deflect 1/64" per inch of span. For a 32" span, deflect 1/2". Force value is per belt/rib — multiply by rib count for multi-rib belts.
Minimum pulley diameters (examples)
Belt type A: 3.00" pitch dia. · B: 5.40" · 3V: 2.65" · 5V: 7.10". Undersized pulleys dramatically increase belt tension and shorten belt life.
Installation method
Rolling a belt on causes low tension (slip, heat). Prying damages internal cables. Neither method should be used — use proper tensioning tools.
Field tip: Belts lose tension over time even when correctly installed — high-quality belts still lose nearly 50% of installed tension over their life. Re-check tension during PMs, don't assume it holds.

Applies to pillow block/flange block bearings on pumps, fans, and blower shafts. Oil film within the grease (often thinner than 0.0001") is what actually prevents metal-to-metal contact — it's not just "packing grease in."

Before relubricating
Check the manual for the specific grease type, fill level, and interval — wrong or incompatible grease causes problems down the line
Prep
Clean the grease fitting, surrounding area, and gun nozzle before greasing — contamination is a leading cause of premature bearing failure
Best practice
Relubricate with the bearing in motion when possible — rotation helps distribute grease through the cavity and reduces the risk of blowing a seal on hard start after overfilling
Overfilling risk
Grease concentrated in one spot + hard start = rapid internal pressure spike = failed seal
Temporary heat rise
Normal and expected right after relubrication, even though lubrication's purpose is reducing heat — not a fault by itself
Hard-to-reach bearings
Single-point lubricators or solid/polymer (permanent) lubrication are options when manual greasing isn't practical
Field tip: Low grease levels don't just risk failure eventually — they guarantee excessive wear happening right now. Get a schedule on the calendar rather than relubricating reactively.

Oxygen in the air combines with copper to form surface oxide (light-to-dark brown discoloration). At brazing temperature a heavier black "cupric oxide" forms, which flakes off as scale that circulates through the system and can restrict flow through metering devices or reversing valve pilot capillary tubes.

Why it matters more now
POE oils (used with R-410A and other HFCs) have a solvent effect and can "scrub" tube walls, circulating oxide and scale that mineral oil (R-22 era) didn't
Procedure
Flow dry nitrogen through the tube during brazing to displace oxygen and prevent scale formation
Starting flow rate
2–3 cfh or 1.5–2 psi. Some techs set pressure until a slight flow is felt at the exit point on the back of the hand
Timing
Initiate flow before heating and continue until the part has cooled
Avoid excessive flow
High flow rate cools the tube (reducing brazing heat efficiency) and can reduce braze alloy penetration. A small hole in an end cap lets nitrogen escape without building pressure
Field tip: Test-braze on the bench and section the finished assembly to inspect for a clean inner-tube wall before relying on a given flow rate setting in the field.

Typical target
350–450 CFM per ton (400 CFM/ton ideal). Example: 3-ton system → ~1,200 CFM target
Temperature split (ΔT)
Return − supply, typically 18–22°F for cooling
Heating airflow check
CFM = BTU output ÷ (1.08 × ΔT). Example: 80,000 BTU / (1.08 × 55°F) ≈ 1,360 CFM
Low airflow signs
Frozen coil, high head/low suction, hot spots, long run times
High airflow signs
Low temperature split, noisy ducts, poor humidity control
Field tip: Verify airflow before charging or adjusting refrigerant. Below 350 CFM/ton, fix airflow first — refrigerant readings will be misleading.
Heat rejection
Air-cooled: direct to outdoor air via coils/fans. Water-cooled: to a water loop, cooled by tower/fluid cooler
Efficiency
Air-cooled drops in hot weather. Water-cooled is higher efficiency, more stable, especially at scale
Maintenance
Air-cooled: coil cleaning, fan motors. Water-cooled: water treatment, basin cleaning, pump seals, tube cleaning
Common applications
Air-cooled: small-medium RTUs, splits, VRF. Water-cooled: large commercial/industrial, process cooling, data centers
Cooling method
DX: refrigerant absorbs heat directly at the evaporator coil. Chilled water: water cooled by a chiller, circulated to AHU coils
System type
DX: closed refrigerant circuit, self-contained per unit. Chilled water: central plant, multiple AHUs share one loop
Chilled water supply/return
Typically 42–45°F supply, 55–58°F return
Applications
DX: small-medium buildings, RTUs, splits, VRF. Chilled water: large commercial, industrial, hospital, campus
Key component
Heat pump has a reversing valve to switch heating/cooling. Straight AC has none — fixed refrigerant flow direction
Heating method
Heat pump extracts heat from outdoor air. Straight AC relies on a separate furnace or electric heat
Control wiring
Heat pump includes O/B terminal for reversing valve + aux heat. Straight AC has no O/B terminal
Defrost
Heat pumps need an automatic defrost cycle. Straight AC doesn't.
Control type
TXV: mechanical, sensing bulb pressure. Fixed orifice: fixed hole size, flow varies with pressure. EEV: electronic, stepper motor/solenoid via board feedback
Superheat control
TXV: maintains constant SH at evap outlet. Fixed orifice: no control, SH varies widely. EEV: precisely maintains target SH electronically
Responsiveness
TXV: moderate (mechanical delay). Fixed orifice: slow. EEV: fast and accurate
Typical applications
TXV: standard splits/commercial. Fixed orifice: budget/residential. EEV: VRF/VRV, chillers, modern inverter-driven systems
Normal ΔT — cooling
Residential 18–22°F, commercial 16–20°F
Normal ΔT — heating
Gas furnace 40–70°F, heat pump 20–30°F, hydronic coil 25–40°F
Low ΔT cooling
Possible undercharge, low airflow, or inefficient compressor. Low ΔT + high superheat = low charge
High ΔT cooling
Restricted airflow, dirty filters, or undersized return duct
Evaporator coil clues
Even frost = normal. Frost only at metering device = restriction. Ice across whole coil = low airflow
Field tip: Low ΔT + high amps = airflow issue. Low ΔT + low amps = refrigerant or compressor issue.
