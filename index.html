Mitsubishi factory procedure for outdoor unit compressor/accumulator replacement. Licensed HVAC professionals only — refer to full manufacturer instructions before attempting.

1. Recover charge
Both high and low side service ports. Current models may lack L-shaped adapters — use valve fittings instead. Heat may be needed at accumulator/compressor to fully recover charge; never use open flame.
2–5. Access
Remove exterior panels, compressor compartment access cover, and top cover (may require removing blanking cover, transformer box, and lowering the electrical panel depending on model)
6–7. De-braze & unmount
De-braze suction and discharge line joints closest to the compressor (or accumulator joints if replacing that). Remove compressor mounting bolt nuts and anti-vibration bracket — one nut is normally missing by design. Remove crankcase heater/thermistor if equipped.
8–10. Oil check
Pour oil from suction pipe into a graduated container. Less than 1 liter (32 oz) or any foreign matter → consult Mitsubishi rep. Acid-test the oil to determine correct filter core if installing a PAC-SPRFCS filter kit.
11–14. Install & braze
Install new compressor/accumulator, filter kit if used, braze all joints while purging with nitrogen at 0.5–2 PSI, reinstall mounting hardware and crankcase heater
15–20. Pressure test & evacuate
Pressurize to 600 PSI with nitrogen and leak check. Evacuate to 4000 microns, break with nitrogen, re-evacuate to 1500 then 500 microns. Hold 500 microns for 30 min pump time, then 1 hour standing.
21. Recharge
Weigh in charge as liquid to the high side only, never the low side. New accumulator requires oil added to the suction line before the accumulator via a two-port oil charge tank.
22–23. Reset compressor hours
Read/reset via dip switches on the control board — switch sequence differs between J-generation and K/L-generation units. Confirm hours read back to zero after reset.
Field tip: Note the missing mounting nut on step 7 is normal, not a prior tech's mistake — don't waste time hunting for a 4th nut that was never there.

Amps at/above RLA
Hot discharge + liquid line at cond outlet → high head. Check condenser fan amps/airflow before blaming charge.
Amps below RLA
Warm-to-cool suction, reduced discharge temp → undercharge or worn valves. Sharp cold spot on liquid line = restriction instead.

High ΔT (above ~24°F)

Frost migrating past coil onto suction line
Overfeeding — bad TXV, or straight overcharge on a piston system.

SH is the controlled variable on a TXV — it holds unless charge is way off. SC is the primary charge indicator.

Overcharge
Amps at/above RLA, liquid line and discharge line hot to the touch, frost pattern normal. Gauges will confirm: head high, SC high, SH normal-to-low.
Undercharge
Amps below RLA, suction line warm, liquid line uniformly warm. Gauges will confirm: both pressures low, SH high, SC low.
Liquid line restriction
Amps starved, sharp cold spot at the drier or a fitting. Gauges will confirm: sharp pressure/temp drop across the restriction, SH high. See Flash gas under HVAC Fundamentals for why this restriction causes the symptoms it does.
Evap overfeeding / failing TXV
Frost migrating onto the suction line, low ΔT. Gauges will confirm: SH low/near zero, suction pressure high for the load.

CLO stands for Current-Sensing Lock Out (also called Compressor Lock Out). It prevents restart of the compressor/unit after a safety trip until power is momentarily interrupted to the CLO. Applies to Carrier 38/48/50 series units.

Normal operating condition
18–30 VAC at Terminals 1 & 2, with 2.5A through the current sensor loop — relay stays de-energized
Trip condition
Current drops to 0.5A → relay energizes within 1 second, opens N.C. contact between 2 & 3, closes N.C. contact between 2 & X (alarm signal)
What trips it
HPS (high pressure switch), LPS (low pressure switch), or FPT (freeze protection thermostat) opening in the C1 compressor contactor circuit
Field tip: A compressor that won't restart after a nuisance trip, with no obvious fault present, is often just waiting on the CLO to be reset by cycling power — check the CLO before condemning the compressor or contactor.
Unit-specific — confirm with current manufacturer service manual for the exact model.

Systematic fuse-isolation method for a resettable low-voltage fuse that keeps tripping.

1. Verify power source
Check transformer secondary (24–28 VAC). No 24V → check primary, repair/replace transformer.
2. Remove all thermostat wires
Reset fuse, power up. Trips → short is inside the unit (go to step 3). Holds → short is in the stat/cable, reconnect one wire at a time.
3. Isolate components one at a time
Disconnect (in order): gas valve, high-limit/aux safeties, economizer/OA damper harness, blower relay coil, condenser contactor coil. Reset fuse before each test. Fuse holds after disconnecting X → X is shorting.
4. Still trips with everything disconnected
Inspect harness for rubbed wires on blower deck, pinched wires near gas valve bracket, wires touching heat exchanger, or burned transformer secondary touching chassis.
5. Confirm
Reconnect components one at a time until fuse trips again — the last one reconnected is the fault.

Unloaders reduce compressor capacity by isolating a cylinder bank from the suction manifold, letting the unloaded cylinders spin without pumping refrigerant. Two control types on Carrier reciprocating compressors: electric (remote signal to a solenoid) and pressure-operated (driven by suction/discharge differential).

Electric — loaded (de-energized)
Valve passageways aligned for loaded conditions. No manual adjustment required.
Electric — unloaded (energized)
Solenoid opens the capacity control valve port, venting discharge gas from behind the unloader piston back to suction — isolates that cylinder bank, no compression
Pressure-operated — loaded
Suction pressure overcomes control set point spring → poppet valve closes → discharge gas builds against unloader piston → bank runs fully loaded
Pressure-operated — unloaded
Suction pressure drops below set point → diaphragm snaps, opens poppet → discharge gas vents to suction side → cylinder bank isolated
Control set point range
0–86 psig, adjustable. Each full turn CCW on the adjustment nut lowers load-up point ~7.2 psi
Pressure differential range
6–16 psi between load-up and unload points. Each full turn CW on the adjustment screw increases differential ~0.8 psi
Startup behavior
On pressure-operated units, controlled cylinders don't load until suction/discharge differential reaches approx 25 psig
Field check
Solenoid should give an audible click when actuated (electric type). No click with 24V present — suspect solenoid coil.
Field tip: Cylinder head assemblies include the capacity control valve — for electrically actuated heads, the solenoid coil is purchased separately. Confirm valve plate gasket thickness (0.32" for 06ER/EY refrigeration models, 0.07" for all others) before reassembly.
Unit-specific — confirm with current manufacturer service manual for the exact model.

Residential techs moving to 5-20 ton light commercial rooftops share a lot with residential equipment, but there's more to check on the bigger stuff.

Fresh air filters
Often metal mesh — wash regularly with a hose and degreaser, especially near exhaust hoods
Belt tension
Tight enough not to slip or vibrate excessively, no tighter — over-tight belts cause high blower amps, breakage, stretching, and excessive bearing wear. The 1/2" deflection rule is a rough guide at best since it ignores belt length and deflection force; a proper tension tool gives a real number
Pulley alignment
Align both pulley centers (left-right) and camber (angle). Misalignment usually traces back to a slipped motor base bolt or a recently adjusted/replaced pulley. Causes thrown/damaged belts, damaged bearings, noise. Worn pulleys (lost shape, grooved sides) cause improper airflow and shorter belt life even when alignment is correct
Condenser splitting
Multi-row condensers often need to be split apart to clean completely — frequency depends on location and airborne debris
Phase balance (3-phase)
Check voltage leg to leg on all three legs. Imbalance over 2% is detrimental, over 4% is unacceptable — even small imbalances cause motor heat and failure. Many 3-phase systems already have phase monitoring controls installed to catch this automatically
Field tip: New commercial techs tend to over-tighten belts out of habit from residential work — if you're chasing high blower amps or premature bearing wear on a rooftop, check belt tension before anything else.

Two compressors sharing one circuit (three = a trio) — common in RTUs, chillers, DOAS, and data center cooling to hit minimum modulation requirements without the expense of a single variable-speed compressor.

Why use them
Splits load across two compressors so the circuit can turn down further (e.g. to 25% of full capacity) than one single-speed compressor could alone — helps meet efficiency standards like IEER/IPLV
OEL (oil equalization line)
Connects the two compressor sumps below the compressors so oil balances between them. Must be level — sloped OEL piping starves one compressor of oil and floods the other, causing premature failure. Copeland tandem-ready compressors ship with spacers to keep it level
TPTL (two-phase transfer line)
Larger line that equalizes both oil and refrigerant, usually with its own sight glass for checking oil level. Most Copeland trios use this. Combined with the OEL, this is a "three-pipe" design
GEL (gas equalization line)
Located near the top of the compressor shell, equalizes pressure between compressors. If present, it's a "four-pipe" design — common on the smallest tandem pairings where a sight glass won't fit
Replacing just one failed compressor
Compressors under 7" diameter or under 10 HP generally require full tandem replacement — most distributors don't stock tandem-ready compressors that small. Look for an oil equalization port and sight glass to confirm a compressor is tandem-ready before ordering a single replacement
When to replace the whole tandem anyway
Contamination from a catastrophic failure of one compressor can compromise the other, since they share oil and refrigerant — even a 9"+ compressor might warrant full tandem replacement in that case
Field tip: Never replace a tandem-ready compressor with a standard (non-tandem) compressor even if the ports look close — a non-tandem compressor has no oil equalization port and cannot be piped into an existing OEL/TPTL/GEL setup.

Prevents liquid refrigerant from migrating into the compressor crankcase during the off-cycle and diluting the oil. A missing or failed crankcase heater is a leading cause of premature compressor failure — and it's not just a cold-climate problem (a hot attic keeping the indoor coil warm while the outdoor unit cools rapidly at sunset causes the same migration in Florida).

What happens without one
Liquid refrigerant floods the crankcase and dilutes the oil. At next startup, the sudden pressure drop makes it boil violently into foam — this causes bearing washout (foam forced out, starving bearings of lubrication) and slugging (incompressible liquid drawn into cylinders/scrolls, breaking valves or shattering scroll sets)
Scroll compressors
Have compliance mechanisms that let scrolls separate slightly to handle small amounts of liquid, but they're not immune — a crankcase heater may be skippable only below a specific charge limit per the manufacturer's data. Never add a hard start kit to a scroll that's stalling on liquid migration; the extra torque can force it to start against a slug and break the scroll set
Variable-speed compressors
Often don't need an external heater at all — the VFD sends small DC current through the motor windings, generating up to 50W of heat directly
Wrap-around (belly-band)
Most common type, straps around the compressor housing. Easy field install/replacement, even heating, but less efficient than internal heaters
Immersion (well) style
Inserts into a dedicated port, heats the oil directly. More efficient and faster, but only works on compressors designed with a well
Installation
Install as low on the shell as possible without covering the bottom weld, perfectly horizontal, snug contact along the full length, avoid other weld seams (creates hot spots). Torque the clamp to 20–25 in-lb. Never power a heater in free air before it's installed — it will burn out
Pre-startup requirement
On a new install or after extended shutdown, energize the heater a minimum of 24 hours before first start — this drives off migrated liquid from the oil, not just warms it. The first start is the most critical one a compressor gets; don't skip this
Diagnosing a failed heater
Visual: cracks, burned spots, frayed leads. Temperature: shell should feel slightly warm when energized, not hot. Resistance: OL means open/failed element (target Ω = voltage² ÷ wattage). Ground: any reading other than OL indicates a short to ground
Wiring method to watch for
When replacing a single-pole contactor with a double-pole, the crankcase heater can lose its power source since a double-pole interrupts both legs. Fix: jumper L2 to T2 to mimic the continuous-voltage behavior the heater was relying on
Field tip: A crankcase heater is one of the most legitimate low-cost upsells in the trade — cheap insurance against a multi-thousand-dollar compressor replacement, and a mark of a thorough tech.

R-410A equipment with a GWP over 700 stopped being manufactured January 1, 2025, and installation of new R-410A split systems becomes illegal January 1, 2026 (chillers and other specialized equipment have later deadlines). Scroll compressor design had to change to handle the new refrigerants.

Common misconception
A2Ls do NOT contain R-290 (propane, an A3 refrigerant). R-32 is a pure refrigerant on its own; R-454B is a blend of 68.9% R-32 and 31.1% R-1234yf — no propane in either
Why compressor design changed
R-32 runs higher discharge temperatures than R-410A, requiring a more viscous oil (POE-46 instead of POE-36) to keep from breaking down under the extra heat. R-454B has minor glide (1–2.5°F) requiring some design accommodation, though nowhere near as much as older R-407C blends (up to 11.5°F)
Copeland model number nomenclature
Traditional A1 scroll models start with Z. A2L/flammable-refrigerant models start with Y — specifically YA for R-454B, YP for R-32
R-410A replacement compressors still available
The manufacturing ban applies to complete systems, not individual parts — Copeland's new LXE line (ZPLXE single-stage, ZPSLXE two-stage) replaces legacy ZP-prefix models for service work, with 80 LXE models covering 277 legacy models
Service practice changes
Minimal if you were already doing things right — nitrogen pressure testing and deep vacuum go from best practice to required, but R-32/R-454B operating pressures are close enough to R-410A that gauge readings and targets don't shift dramatically
Field tip: Don't assume "A2L" means propane is involved — check the actual refrigerant name (R-32, R-454B, etc.) before making assumptions about flammability handling or charge limits.

TXVs are misdiagnosed constantly, usually because a tech fixates on suction pressure alone. Low suction plus adding refrigerant that doesn't raise it looks like a failed valve — but the valve is often just doing exactly what it's designed to do (close further as more liquid becomes available), while the real problem is airflow or load.

Step 1 — confirm liquid supply
Check subcooling first. In theory 1°F is the minimum for a full line of liquid, but in reality confirm the factory-specified subcool, usually around 10°F
Step 2 — confirm pressure differential
Want at least 100 PSI minimum difference between liquid line pressure and desired evaporator pressure. Low ambient conditions dropping head pressure too far can starve the valve of the differential it needs to function
Step 3 — check evaporator outlet superheat
Most A/C systems maintain 6–14°F superheat at the evap outlet once subcool and pressure differential are confirmed adequate. In range = the valve is doing its job, not bad
Below 6°F superheat
Possible overfeeding (failed open) — double check thermometer/gauge calibration first before condemning the valve
Above 14°F superheat with proper subcool/differential confirmed
Failed closed (underfeeding). Check for a blocked inlet screen before assuming the valve itself is bad — freeze the coil intentionally or use thermal imaging to find the actual point of temperature/pressure drop
Overfeeding first check
Bulb insulation, placement, and strapping — if the bulb isn't properly sensing the suction line, the valve stays too far open regardless of anything else
Most common real causes of failure
Contaminants or moisture reaching the valve and sticking/restricting it, bulb tube rub-outs, or an external equalizer without a core depressor installed on a Schrader port
Refrigeration cross-check
Same principle as a sight glass check on a split system — confirm no kinked lines or plugged driers before condemning the valve
Field tip: If you're chasing low suction and adding refrigerant makes the TXV close further without suction climbing, stop — that's the valve doing its job correctly. Go check airflow and load before touching the valve.

Checks a single-phase compressor motor for winding-to-winding shorts (partially shorted windings) after the motor has already been checked for grounded, open, or shorted windings.

Rule of thumb
Resistance of start winding (C to S) should equal 3 to 6 times the resistance of run winding (C to R)
Example — good compressor
Start = 3.0Ω, Run = 0.5Ω → 3.0/0.5 = 6 → good
Example — partially shorted
Start = 1.25Ω, Run = 0.5Ω → 1.25/0.5 = 2.5 → partially shorted (ratio under 3)
Safety
Compressor terminals must not be disturbed while system is pressurized. Do not remove/connect wiring at the compressor unless system pressure is reduced to 0 psig.
Field tip: Use the R×1 ohm scale and zero the meter first — these are extremely low resistance values, and a good compressor can get misjudged as bad with an inaccurate meter setup.

Same basic refrigeration cycle as any DX system — the difference is the evaporator chills water (or glycol) instead of cooling air directly, and that water is then piped out to air handlers/fan coils elsewhere in the building.

Reciprocating
Older/smaller chillers, largely phased out on new equipment but still common in the field. Piston-driven, multiple cylinders, capacity staged by unloading cylinder banks (see Compressor unloaders card under RTU/Package Units)
Scroll
Small-to-mid tonnage chillers, often multiple scrolls manifolded together for capacity steps. Quiet, few moving parts, common on air-cooled packaged chillers
Screw
Mid-to-large tonnage. Twin rotating screws compress refrigerant continuously — smooth capacity modulation via a sliding slide valve, no on/off cylinder banks. Oil management is critical (oil separator, oil cooler)
Centrifugal
Large tonnage (100+ tons), common in big commercial/campus plants. Impeller-driven, most efficient per ton at full load. Often runs refrigerants below atmospheric pressure at the evaporator (R-123, R-1233zd) — requires a purge unit to remove air/non-condensables that leak in
Absorption
No mechanical compressor at all — uses heat (steam, hot water, or direct gas fire) plus a lithium bromide/water absorption cycle to move refrigerant. Rare, but shows up where cheap waste heat or steam is available
Field tip: If you can't tell what compressor type you're looking at, check the nameplate first — screw and centrifugal chillers will call it out directly, and the compressor housing shape is a giveaway (screws are a long cylindrical housing, centrifugals have a distinct volute/impeller housing).
Chilled water flow switch
Prevents the compressor from running with no/low water flow through the evaporator — a flowing evaporator is what keeps the refrigerant from freezing solid. Nuisance trips are usually a dirty strainer, air in the system, or a failed pump, not the switch itself
Low temp / freeze protection cutout
Second layer of protection behind the flow switch — trips on low leaving water temp or low refrigerant temp/pressure before the evaporator can freeze and split. Never bypass this to keep a unit running
Why evaporators freeze
Low water flow, low charge, or a failing TXV/EEV all drop evaporator temp below 32°F at the tube wall — ice expands and can crack copper tubes, turning a service call into a full evaporator replacement
High/low pressure safeties
Same purpose as any DX system — protect the compressor from operating outside its envelope. On larger machines these are often electronic (transducer + control board) rather than mechanical switches
Purge unit (low-pressure centrifugal only)
Since the evaporator runs below atmospheric pressure, air and moisture leak in over time rather than refrigerant leaking out. The purge unit periodically pulls this air off and vents it, keeping non-condensables from building up and killing capacity/efficiency
Staging / lead-lag (multi-chiller plants)

This is the concept that makes the approach/wet-bulb numbers elsewhere in this section make sense. A cooling tower doesn't cool water by blowing air past it like a condenser coil — it cools water by evaporating a small portion of it.

Why evaporation cools
Turning liquid water into vapor takes a large amount of heat (latent heat of vaporization) — that heat gets pulled from the remaining water, cooling it. This is the same principle as sweat cooling skin
Why wet bulb, not dry bulb, is the limit
Because the process depends on evaporation, a tower's performance is limited by how much moisture the air can still absorb — that's what wet-bulb temperature measures. A tower can cool water close to (but never below) the entering air's wet-bulb temp, regardless of the dry-bulb (regular thermometer) temp outside
Why this matters in humid weather
High humidity means wet-bulb temp is close to dry-bulb temp, so the air can't absorb much more moisture — tower performance drops even if it doesn't feel especially hot outside. This is normal, not a fault
Water loss
Evaporation constantly loses water to the atmosphere — this is why towers need continuous makeup water and a float valve, unlike a sealed closed-loop system
Field tip: If a customer asks why the tower "isn't cooling as well" on a humid day, this is usually the answer — check approach temp against wet bulb, not against the air temp on your phone's weather app.
Splash fill
Water splashes over stacked bars/grids, breaking into droplets to increase surface area for evaporation. More open, less prone to clogging, but lower efficiency than film fill
Film fill
Water flows as a thin film over closely spaced PVC sheets. More surface area and more efficient than splash fill, but fouls more easily — needs cleaner water and is more sensitive to scaling/algae
Drift eliminators
Baffles at the air discharge that catch water droplets carried by the airflow, so the tower loses water only to evaporation, not to droplets blowing out with the exhaust air. Damaged/missing eliminators show up as excessive water loss and wet surfaces/staining near the tower discharge
Fan drive types
Belt-driven (adjustable via sheave, most common on smaller towers), gear-driven (right-angle gearbox, common on larger induced-draft towers, needs its own oil service), and direct-drive (motor shaft-mounted straight to the fan, fewer wear points)
Induced vs forced draft
Induced draft: fan at the top pulls air up through the fill (most common). Forced draft: fan at the bottom pushes air in — more prone to recirculating warm discharge air back into the intake
VFD fan control
Modulates fan speed to hold a target leaving water temp instead of just cycling on/off — saves energy and reduces belt/bearing wear from constant starts. See Mounted bearing lubrication under Motor & Drive Reference for gearbox/fan shaft bearing service
Field tip: Excessive water loss with no obvious leak is almost always drift eliminators — check them before assuming a piping or basin leak.

Open cooling towers create warm water aerosolized into the air — exactly the conditions Legionella bacteria need to grow and spread. Water treatment on a tower isn't just about protecting equipment, it's a health and safety issue.

Why it matters
Legionnaires' disease spreads through inhaled mist from contaminated water sources — poorly maintained cooling towers are a documented source of outbreaks. Many states/municipalities require registered testing and maintenance plans for open towers
Biocide
Kills bacteria/algae in the water — needs to be maintained at proper levels continuously, not just shocked occasionally
Cycles of concentration / blowdown
As water evaporates, dissolved minerals concentrate in the remaining water. Blowdown (bleeding off some tower water and replacing with fresh makeup) keeps mineral concentration and conductivity from climbing too high and scaling the fill/coils
pH
Typically maintained 7.0–9.0. Out-of-range pH accelerates scaling (high) or corrosion (low)
If a tower has sat idle
Towers that have been offline (seasonal shutdown, unoccupied building) need to be disinfected before restart per manufacturer/local requirements — stagnant warm water is a higher Legionella risk than water in continuous circulation
Field tip: If a building's water treatment program looks neglected (no test records, cloudy/slimy basin water), flag it — this is a liability issue for the customer, not just a maintenance one.

Low voltage wiring
Typically ≤50V, most common is 24VAC in HVAC (thermostats, controls, safeties). Carries control/signal current, not load current. "Safer" than line voltage but still causes damage or comm failures if poorly wired. Sensitive to EMI near power lines/motors — run separate from high voltage, shield if needed. Match voltage type (24VAC vs 12/24VDC) — mismatch damages components
Relay basics
Coil (receives the low voltage signal) → armature → contacts (open/close) → spring (returns to rest state). A relay bridges low voltage control to a high voltage load — e.g. a 24V thermostat signal energizes a relay coil, which switches 240V to the compressor
EMR (electromechanical)
Physical contacts — the traditional relay type, visible/audible click on operation
SSR (solid state)
No moving contacts — faster switching, silent, more reliable long-term, higher cost
Latching relay
Holds its position with power removed — power-saving design, common where the load needs to stay set without continuous coil power
Time delay relay
Controlled on/off delay, used in sequencing (e.g. staggering compressor starts, blower-off delay)
Contact rating
Must match the load — underrated contacts arc, weld shut, and fail early
Coil voltage
Must match the control signal exactly, including AC vs DC — a DC coil on an AC signal (or vice versa) won't operate correctly even if the voltage number looks right
Inductive loads
Motors and solenoids need spike protection (snubber circuits) — the collapsing magnetic field on de-energization generates a voltage spike that can damage contacts/electronics without one
Isolation
Relays provide electrical isolation between the control circuit and the load circuit — this is a safety function, not just a switching convenience
Field tip: A relay that hums or chatters instead of pulling in cleanly is usually low coil voltage or a worn/misaligned armature — check coil voltage first before condemning the relay.

Delta T is one piece of the diagnostic puzzle — not a standalone verdict. The 20°F rule of thumb is a starting point, not a target to force a system into.

Formula
ΔT = T2 − T1, measured across the evaporator (return air temp minus supply air temp)
Probe placement is critical
Take the supply reading just above the air handler, not at a register — leaky, torn, or poorly insulated ducts add unwanted gains between the coil and the register that corrupt the reading. Return reading should be equally representative, not right next to a leak
20°F is a rule of thumb, not universal
Most equipment falls in the 16–20°F range at 400 CFM/ton, but manufacturers vary target delta T by equipment design. Chasing 20°F on a system designed for 15°F just makes things worse
Airflow effect
System capacity (BTU absorption) doesn't change with fan speed — only how much air passes through the coil does. Decreased airflow = high split. Increased airflow = low split. This is predictable and gives you a logical starting point when delta T is off
Humidity effect
High humidity air is less dense (water vapor molecules are lighter than air), meaning less sensible heat capacity per cubic foot, plus latent heat removal (condensing moisture) draws energy away from the sensible heat exchange — both push delta T lower on humid days, even on a correctly operating system
Refrigerant charge
Delta T is NOT a reliable charge-verification test on its own — you can read 20°F on a badly charged system and 12°F on a properly charged one just as easily. Use it alongside suction/head pressure, superheat, and subcool, not instead of them
350 vs 400 CFM/ton systems
In humid climates, some systems are deliberately set to 350 CFM/ton to extract more moisture — this makes standard delta T charts/calculators inapplicable, since the slower airflow allows more heat absorption and reads higher than the standard chart expects
Field tip: If you're only checking delta T at the return grille and a random supply register, you're not really measuring delta T — you're measuring duct leakage plus delta T. Get the supply probe right at the air handler outlet.

This is what "low compression ratio is the tell" (see Compressor mechanical & non-condensables under RTU/Package Units) is referring to — a concept worth understanding on its own, not just as a phrase. Applies to any compressor, not just RTUs.

Formula
Compression ratio = absolute discharge pressure ÷ absolute suction pressure. Absolute = gauge pressure + atmospheric (14.7 psi at sea level)
Worked example (R-22)
240 psig discharge + 14.7 = 254.7 psia. 75 psig suction + 14.7 = 89.7 psia. 254.7 ÷ 89.7 = 2.84:1
Typical AC range
2.3:1 to 3.5:1 — modern high-efficiency equipment trends toward the low end of this range
Medium-temp refrigeration (cooler)
3.0:1 to 5.5:1 typical (R-404A example)
Low-temp freezer (0°F to -10°F)
6.0:1 to 13.0:1 typical (R-404A example)
Ratio higher than designed
Compressor overheating, oil breakdown, high power consumption, low capacity
Ratio lower than designed
Possible indication of mechanical failure and poor compression — this is the low-CR compressor valve wear signature
Reducing high compression ratio
Keep setpoints at or above design temp for the equipment (don't run a -10°F freezer at -20°F), keep condenser coils clean, maintain proper evaporator airflow, install condensers in shaded/well-ventilated areas
Field tip: A customer setting a thermostat unusually low, or a freezer working hard on a hot day, will show a high compression ratio that isn't a fault by itself — know the expected range for the application before treating it as a problem.

Follow the power, don't hopscotch around the unit. This is the same discipline whether it's a residential split system or a commercial RTU.

Safety first
LOTO whenever working in a commercial setting or out of sight of the disconnect. Never trust a disconnect blade — verify 0V between L1-L2, L1-Ground, and L2-Ground before touching anything
The schematic is your map
Trace the circuit mentally before your meter leads touch a terminal. If you don't know the sequence of operation, you're guessing, not troubleshooting
Diagnostic toolkit
True RMS multimeter (required for accurate readings on VFDs/ECM motors), amp clamp, fused jumper wires (3–5A, for safely bypassing controls to isolate circuits), megohmmeter for compressor winding-to-ground checks
Step 1 — high voltage
Measure at the disconnect, then line side of the contactor, then load side (T1/T2) if pulled in. 240V in but 200V out = pitted contacts creating resistance — replace the contactor
Step 2 — low voltage
If high voltage is present but the unit won't run, check R to C at the transformer secondary for 24V. Then check Y to C on a cooling call — 24V there means the thermostat is doing its job and the problem is downstream
Step 3 — isolating a dead short
If the low-voltage fuse blows immediately on a call, stop wasting fuses. Disconnect the Y wire going outside and restore power. Fuse holds → short is in the outdoor low-voltage wiring or contactor coil. Fuse still blows → short is inside (board, rubbed thermostat wire, bad transformer) — break the circuit down component by component
Field tip: If you're not sure where to start, start at the source and work toward the load — every step you skip is a guess you'll have to walk back later.

A capacitor can test good cold on the bench and still fail once it's hot and under load — a bench MFD test alone isn't the full picture.

Capacitor load test
With the system running, measure amps on the start wire (common/HERM to compressor S terminal) and voltage across the capacitor (C to HERM)
Formula
µF = (Amps × 2652) ÷ Voltage. The constant 2652 is specific to 60Hz power
Tolerance
If the running result is outside ±6% or ±10% of the label rating (check which tolerance applies to that cap), replace it — even if it looks physically fine
Contactor voltage drop test
With the system running (contactor closed), measure voltage across each pole: L1 to T1, then L2 to T2
Contactor pass/fail
Good contacts read near 0V, ideally <0.1V. More than 0.5V across closed contacts means that resistance is generating heat and starving the compressor of voltage — replace it. A visual check alone (ants, light burns) isn't definitive
Field tip: If you find a failed capacitor, don't just swap it and move on — check condenser coil cleanliness and fan amp draw. Excess heat is a leading cause of capacitor failure, and a fresh cap on a dirty coil just buys time before the next one fails too.

When a compressor won't start, don't assume it's dead — distinguish the actual failure mode before condemning it.

Winding resistance check — single-phase (PSC)
Applies to single-phase compressors with Common/Run/Start terminals — most residential/light commercial reciprocating and single-phase scroll compressors. Measure resistance between C-R, C-S, and S-R. On a healthy compressor: R(C-R) + R(C-S) ≈ R(S-R)
Winding resistance check — three-phase (scroll / semi-hermetic)
Three-phase compressors don't have a common/run/start configuration — they have three separate windings. Measure across all three terminal pairs (T1-T2, T2-T3, T1-T3). On a healthy compressor, all three readings should be roughly equal to each other, not summed. A significantly unequal reading among the three points to a winding fault.
Ground check
Meter on the highest ohm scale (or a megger) from each terminal to clean copper ground. Any reading other than OL (infinite) indicates winding insulation breakdown
Locked rotor vs mechanical seize
Check LRA on the data plate. Pulls LRA and trips the breaker instantly → mechanically seized. Pulls significantly less than LRA but hums and trips on thermal overload → possible single-phasing or a loose connection, not a seized compressor
Field tip: Don't skip the ground check even when winding resistance looks fine — a compressor can have good winding-to-winding resistance and still be shorted to the shell.

The hardest calls are the ones that work when you arrive and fail when you leave. These patterns explain a lot of them.

Ghost voltage
Reading 40–80V on a disconnected wire is often induced voltage from adjacent live wires, not a real short. Switch the meter to Low-Z (Low Impedance) mode — if the voltage drops to zero, it's a ghost. If it holds, it's a live short
The rub-out
Check the low-voltage wire bundle where it enters the outdoor unit cabinet. Vibration rubs insulation raw against the refrigerant line or sheet metal, causing a short that only happens when the unit vibrates — won't show up sitting still
Loose neutrals
In the air handler, check the white neutral wire bundle. A loose neutral can cause bizarre voltage readings (like 60V showing on a 120V circuit) and erratic motor behavior that looks unrelated to the actual cause
Field tip: If a fault only shows up under vibration or load, don't just re-torque connections and call it fixed — flex the harness by hand while metering to try to reproduce the fault before you leave.

In a series circuit, total resistance is just the sum of every load's resistance. In parallel, it's trickier — and it goes down, not up, as you add more loads.

Formula
1÷Rt = 1÷R1 + 1÷R2 + 1÷R3 (add as many loads as are actually in parallel)
Worked example
R1=120Ω, R2=45Ω, R3=360Ω. 1÷120=0.0083, 1÷45=0.022, 1÷360=0.0028. Sum = 0.0331. 1÷0.0331 = 30.21Ω total — less than the lowest individual resistance in the circuit
Why total resistance drops
Each additional parallel path gives current another way through — more paths means less overall resistance, which by Ohm's law means higher total amperage
Real-world example
Compressor amps + condenser fan amps = total condenser circuit amps. This is parallel circuit behavior you already measure every day
Myth to drop
Electricity doesn't only take the path of least resistance — it takes all available paths. Every additional parallel path just lowers the overall resistance between the two points, which is why a breaker trips when you add another load to an already-loaded circuit
Field tip: If you need the resistance of just two loads in parallel, there's a shortcut: (R1 × R2) ÷ (R1 + R2) — same answer as the full formula, less arithmetic.
Full reference (diagrams, related series-circuit article): Parallel Circuit Resistance — HVAC School

A single-phase motor has three terminals (common, start, run) but only two windings. Common is just the shared point between them — which is why amperage is always measured on common, since that reads the combined current of both windings.

Why Ohm's law doesn't apply cleanly here
AC voltage/amperage isn't fixed (we measure RMS, not peak), and impedance in an inductive load isn't fixed either — it combines static winding resistance with inductive reactance that builds as the magnetic field expands/collapses and back EMF as the motor spins
Winding ohm readings are nearly meaningless
Static resistance readings are so low that meter inaccuracy becomes a real factor. The real resistance (impedance) only shows up once the motor is energized with AC and the magnetic fields are interacting
The run winding
Connected directly across the line. Current is high at start (low impedance) and drops as the motor spins up and impedance increases — this is where essentially ALL the true inrush current occurs
The start winding
Connected through the run capacitor, not directly across the line. Its current is capped by the capacitor's capacitance — it literally cannot draw more current than the capacitor allows, regardless of winding impedance
What this means for inrush readings
Nearly all inrush happens on the run winding, not the start winding. Start winding current doesn't rise until run winding current is already coming back down
Testing it yourself
Measure running amps on the start wire with a capacitor slightly oversized, slightly undersized, and removed entirely — you'll see higher amps, lower amps, then none. That's the capacitor acting as a current ceiling, not the winding
Field tip: Don't try to explain start winding amperage with static ohm readings and Ohm's law alone — the capacitor is doing the current-limiting, not the winding resistance.

Oxygen in the air combines with copper to form surface oxide (light-to-dark brown discoloration). At brazing temperature a heavier black "cupric oxide" forms, which flakes off as scale that circulates through the system and can restrict flow through metering devices or reversing valve pilot capillary tubes.

Why it matters more now
POE oils (used with R-410A and other HFCs) have a solvent effect and can "scrub" tube walls, circulating oxide and scale that mineral oil (R-22 era) didn't
Procedure
Flow dry nitrogen through the tube during brazing to displace oxygen and prevent scale formation
Starting flow rate
2–3 cfh or 1.5–2 psi. Some techs set pressure until a slight flow is felt at the exit point on the back of the hand
Timing
Initiate flow before heating and continue until the part has cooled
Avoid excessive flow
High flow rate cools the tube (reducing brazing heat efficiency) and can reduce braze alloy penetration. A small hole in an end cap lets nitrogen escape without building pressure
Field tip: Test-braze on the bench and section the finished assembly to inspect for a clean inner-tube wall before relying on a given flow rate setting in the field.
Cut & clean
Cut tube square (tube cutter or hacksaw with a sawing fixture), ream/deburr inside and out. Joint surfaces must be clean and free of oil/grease/oxide — stainless wire brush or emery cloth, solvent for oil/grease, then wipe away any grit before assembly
Joint clearance
Hold .002"–.006" at brazing temperature (varies by alloy) — too loose or too tight both cause problems, this is not a "press-fit is fine" situation
Heating sequence
Heat the tube first, working the flame alternately around tube and fitting until both reach brazing temperature, before applying filler metal. On vertical joints, heat the tube first then the fitting, keeping the flame directed toward the fitting
Applying alloy
Only add filler metal once base metals are at brazing temperature. Feed into the joint, not the flame. Stop once the joint is filled — excess fillets waste material and don't improve joint quality
Flux behavior as a heat guide
Flux bubbles as it heats, then goes clear and quiet around 1100°F/593°C — that's your visual cue the joint is close to brazing temperature
Cleanup
Remove all flux residue after brazing — quench or wet-brush while still warm, emery cloth/wire brush if needed. Flux left in place is corrosive over time
Field tip: If the outside of the joint is hot but the alloy won't flow, the inside isn't actually up to temperature yet — heat the tube first so heat conducts into the fitting, not just the fitting surface.
Oxy-acetylene
Use a neutral-to-slightly-carburizing flame (well-defined inner cone). Avoid an oxidizing flame — it promotes oxide formation right when you're trying to prevent it
Air-acetylene
Set delivery pressure 14–15 psi if the tank has a delivery gauge. Open the torch valve about 3/4 turn for sufficient gas flow — don't try to throttle the flame size with the torch handle valve, change tip size instead
Sizing the flame to the joint
Air-fuel gives a wider heat pattern than oxy-acetylene, which helps larger tube diameters since heat wraps around the pipe. Oxy-acetylene gives the hottest, most focused heat zone
Field tip: If you're constantly adjusting the torch handle valve mid-joint to control heat, you're using the wrong tip size for that connection — swap tips instead of fighting the flame.
Copper-to-copper
Phos-copper alloys (Stay-Silv 5/15, Dynaflow) — self-fluxing on copper, no external flux needed for a straight copper joint
Copper-to-brass
Same phos-copper alloys work, but now require Stay-Silv white brazing flux — the self-fluxing property only applies to bare copper-to-copper
Ferrous metals (steel, stainless)
Never use phosphorus-bearing alloys on steel or other ferrous metals — it forms a brittle phosphide prone to cracking. Use a high-silver alloy (Safety-Silv 45/56) with white flux instead; black flux for stainless or long/localized heating cycles
Soldering (Stay-Brite) vs brazing
Stay-Brite silver-bearing solder (430–535°F) is a legitimate lower-temperature alternative to brazing on many joints — no oxide scale forms at that temp, so no nitrogen purge is needed. Trade-off: post-braze burst strength on a real brazed joint (1,800 PSI) is roughly half of pre-heat tube strength, while a properly soldered Stay-Brite joint keeps close to full tube strength since there's no annealing from high heat
Aluminum
Requires its own alloys (Al-Braze/Alcor), not standard copper alloys. Aluminum-to-dissimilar-metal joints can be subject to galvanic corrosion — flag this on repairs
Field tip: Getting phos-copper alloy on a steel fitting by habit is an easy mistake when you're used to copper-to-copper all day — check what you're actually joining before reaching for the rod.

Air-cooled: direct to outdoor air via coils/fans. Water-cooled: to a water loop, cooled by tower/fluid cooler
Efficiency
Air-cooled drops in hot weather. Water-cooled is higher efficiency, more stable, especially at scale
Maintenance
Air-cooled: coil cleaning, fan motors. Water-cooled: water treatment, basin cleaning, pump seals, tube cleaning
Common applications
Air-cooled: small-medium RTUs, splits, VRF. Water-cooled: large commercial/industrial, process cooling, data centers
Cooling method
DX: refrigerant absorbs heat directly at the evaporator coil. Chilled water: water cooled by a chiller, circulated to AHU coils
System type
DX: closed refrigerant circuit, self-contained per unit. Chilled water: central plant, multiple AHUs share one loop
Chilled water supply/return
Typically 42–45°F supply, 55–58°F return
Applications
DX: small-medium buildings, RTUs, splits, VRF. Chilled water: large commercial, industrial, hospital, campus
Key component
Heat pump has a reversing valve to switch heating/cooling. Straight AC has none — fixed refrigerant flow direction
Heating method
Heat pump extracts heat from outdoor air. Straight AC relies on a separate furnace or electric heat
Control wiring
Heat pump includes O/B terminal for reversing valve + aux heat. Straight AC has no O/B terminal
Defrost
Heat pumps need an automatic defrost cycle. Straight AC doesn't.
Control type
TXV: mechanical, sensing bulb pressure. Fixed orifice: fixed hole size, flow varies with pressure. EEV: electronic, stepper motor/solenoid via board feedback
Superheat control
TXV: maintains constant SH at evap outlet. Fixed orifice: no control, SH varies widely. EEV: precisely maintains target SH electronically
Responsiveness
TXV: moderate (mechanical delay). Fixed orifice: slow. EEV: fast and accurate
Typical applications
TXV: standard splits/commercial. Fixed orifice: budget/residential. EEV: VRF/VRV, chillers, modern inverter-driven systems
Normal ΔT — cooling
Residential 18–22°F, commercial 16–20°F
Normal ΔT — heating
Gas furnace 40–70°F, heat pump 20–30°F, hydronic coil 25–40°F
Low ΔT cooling
Possible undercharge, low airflow, or inefficient compressor. Low ΔT + high superheat = low charge
High ΔT cooling
Restricted airflow, dirty filters, or undersized return duct
Evaporator coil clues
Even frost = normal. Frost only at metering device = restriction. Ice across whole coil = low airflow
Field tip: Low ΔT + high amps = airflow issue. Low ΔT + low amps = refrigerant or compressor issue.
Scroll
Small-medium tonnage, high efficiency, very quiet. Common failures: scroll separation, flooded starts, liquid slugging damage
Reciprocating
Small-medium tonnage, older/budget units. More moving parts (valves, pistons, rods), more wear points, noisier
Screw
Medium-large tonnage, big RTUs/chillers/industrial. Excellent modulation via slide valve or VFD, but higher maintenance (oil management)

Flash gas is just another term for boiling. It's normal and required directly after the metering device and through the evaporator — that's how the coil absorbs heat via latent heat of vaporization. It's only a problem when it happens somewhere it shouldn't.

What causes flashing
A drop in pressure below the saturation point, a rise in temperature above it, or both. In the liquid line this usually means long/undersized lines, kinks, or a clogged filter drier — all of which drop pressure
Preventing it
Keep line lengths and tight bends to a minimum, insulate liquid line runs through hot spaces, keep refrigerant clean/dry with a properly sized drier
Subcooling as headroom
10°+ of subcooling gives the liquid line margin against pressure drop from long line runs — a system with proper subcooling normally won't flash in the liquid line under normal conditions
Bubbles in a sight glass ≠ automatically "flash gas"
Could genuinely be true flash gas in the liquid line. Could also just mean the refrigerant was never fully condensed to liquid in the first place (low charge) — in that case subcool will read 0° even at the condenser outlet
How to tell the difference
True liquid-line flash gas shows measurable subcooling at the condenser outlet, but you still see/hear/measure boiling refrigerant in the liquid line before the metering device
Field tip: Hissing or surging at the liquid line near the evaporator, or bubbles in the sight glass, means check subcool at the condenser first — that single reading tells you whether you're chasing a restriction or a charge problem.

Dry-bulb temperature (a regular thermometer reading) doesn't account for the actual heat content of air or its evaporative cooling potential — wet-bulb and enthalpy fill that gap.

What wet-bulb actually measures
The temperature a damp thermometer bulb reads in a 900 FPM air stream — exactly what a sling psychrometer measures. The bigger the gap between wet-bulb and dry-bulb (wet-bulb depression), the lower the RH and the greater the evaporative cooling effect available
At 100% RH
Dry-bulb, wet-bulb, and dew point are all the same — air is fully saturated, zero evaporative effect available
Enthalpy
Total heat content of the air, in BTUs per lb. Converting lbs of air to CFM lets you calculate a coil's actual heat-moving capacity, or BTU gain/loss over a duct run
Wet-bulb and enthalpy are linked
On a psychrometric chart, wet-bulb and enthalpy lines run together (at 14.7 PSIA) — a given wet-bulb temp corresponds to a specific enthalpy value. Reading this accurately matters: a difference of 28.4 vs 28.6 BTU/lb adds up fast multiplied across a large air mass
Field tip: This is the concept behind the cooling tower approach/wet-bulb numbers elsewhere in this reference — see "How a cooling tower actually works" under Cooling Towers for the applied version.
